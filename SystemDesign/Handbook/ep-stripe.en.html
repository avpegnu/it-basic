Twelve stages explain retries, external uncertainty, webhooks, journals, reconciliation, payouts, security, cells and live migrations, with evidence, inbox and cutover labs.

1. A platform that accepts and explains money movement

Merchant intent

  • Operation keys and account scope identify intended work; amount/card/time alone do not.

External evidence

  • Timeouts do not decide external effects; retain attempt references and resolve authoritative evidence.

Accounting truth

  • Journals need uniqueness, completeness and reconciliation; balanced sums are only one invariant.

This build uses Stripe as context for a merchant/marketplace payment platform. It is a teaching architecture, not a private implementation or SDK-version integration guide. APIs, states and capacity below belong to the example. Read Payments and Digital Wallet for retry, journals, reservations and cross-owner workflows; this chapter focuses on evolution and integration boundaries.

A payment links merchant intent, external execution evidence and internal financial records. A succeeded row proves only its declared contract. Rails have delays, reversals and disputes, so UX distinguishes pending, action required, authorization, capture and payout. Labs never collect cards or move real funds.

2. Distinguish authentication, authorization, capture and settlement

Two axes: experience and evidence

  1. Intent

    Merchant, amount/currency and account/token context.

  2. Action / authorization

    Challenge if needed, then issuer approval/holds under contract.

  3. Capture / settlement

    Separate external evidence and financial events.

  4. Payout / adjustments

    Availability, reserves, returns and audits.

This is a logical lifecycle rather than a universal payment-method sequence.

Authentication verifies a cardholder or principal. Authorization typically approves or holds issuer funds without final transfer. Capture requests collection under method-specific contracts. Settlement clears/moves funds among financial parties; payout sends available balances to merchant/seller banks. Methods may combine or differ from these steps, requiring adapter semantics beyond one approved boolean.

Payment identity spans attempts and challenges; attempt identity pins processor, route and external key. Capture, refund and payout have separate operation identities. Do not reuse keys for changed amounts or invent new keys after timeouts. Collection success does not preclude later refunds/disputes; later workflows do not rewrite the original event out of history.

3. 12 stages: from endpoint to global platform

Each stage has pressures, decisions, trade-offs, next steps, exit criteria and a diagram. The order teaches design rather than asserting private chronology. Security, durable identities and unknown handling belong in the MVP; later stages deepen boundaries as the product grows. Trace requests, inspect components and explain faults at each external call.

First API and one processor

Start with a merchant-scoped payment operation, amount/currency, payment-method token and state machine. One processor adapter handles external calls, while hosted collection avoids spreading raw PAN. Persist attempts before side effects and represent unknown outcomes from day one; correctness is not an optional later upgrade.

Problem pressure: Merchants want few integration steps, but networks can lose responses after processing.

Architectural decision: Use a modular API/worker, transactional operation store and an adapter with explicit retry/status contracts.

Costs and trade-offs: One processor is a shared dependency; a synchronous API cannot make every payment method immediate.

When is the next step needed?: Client retries and concurrent submits require stable intent identity rather than amount/time guesses.

Trace operations to attempts and processor references; preserve unknown on timeouts rather than inventing failure.

No card data or real rails are used; this is a teaching design.

Scope idempotency by merchant/principal, operation type and key. Fingerprint canonical payloads to reject changed amounts. Atomically persist claims, operation/attempt identity and outbox intent, or use an equivalent recovery protocol. An eternal in-flight marker is not crash recovery.

When is the next step needed?: Outcomes arrive after API responses and need merchant notification.

Auditable journals and reconciliation

Journals record balanced postings by currency, transaction and economic event. Corrections use reversals/adjustments. Balanced does not mean complete: missing or duplicated balanced transactions can pass sum checks. Reconciliation matches journals to processor/bank evidence by identity, amounts, timing and currency, opening owned, aged exceptions.

Problem pressure: Fees, refunds, reserves and settlement timing make balances unexplained.

Architectural decision: Use immutable journals, unique economic identities, atomic postings and reconciliation cases.

Costs and trade-offs: More postings, indexes and close/reopen policies; balance projections still need consistency contracts.

When is the next step needed?: Fraud pressure requires decisions without coupling correctness to model uptime.

Replay preserves unique events; currency sums balance and aged unmatched items have explanations.

Nonzero clearing is not automatically erroneous; account for in-flight work and maturity.

Pin merchants to authoritative cells

Cells bound failures and ownership for account sets, not just regions. Routers use authenticated account mappings and epochs; timeouts do not justify sending one payment’s writes to another cell. Shared identity, config and routers can retain global blast radius. Placement policies include replicas, logs, backups and analytics.

Problem pressure: A deployment or hot merchant should not affect all accounts; placement constraints may vary.

Architectural decision: Use stable routing, cell-local authority/quotas and progressive releases, with frozen, drained and fenced ownership transfers.

Costs and trade-offs: Cross-cell reports and marketplaces become distributed; residency is policy-specific and not proved by a diagram.

When is the next step needed?: Customer authentication can outlast one HTTP request and must resume in the correct cell.

Boundedly reject/redirect wrong-cell traffic; outages and rollbacks never create two authorities.

Cells limit blast radius only with shared dependencies and deployment controls considered.

4. Size accepted work, attempts and backlogs separately

Assume a hundred million payment operations/day, an eightfold peak, four journal postings and three events per operation. Average rate is roughly 1,157 operations/second, peak about 9,259. This implies around 37,037 postings/second and 27,778 initial notifications/second before retries, other workflows and indexes. Monetary volume does not establish transaction rate without amount distributions and time intervals.

At steady non-overloaded 0.4-second mean connector occupancy, 9,259 operations/second imply roughly 3,704 concurrent calls by Little’s law. Four-second latency would imply about 37,037 in flight if arrivals remain unchanged, motivating admission/bulkheads instead of infinite queues. Provider limits and tenant skew may bind first. Mean estimates do not predict p99 or transient backlog growth.

At one KB of metadata/result references per operation for one day, raw state is around 100 decimal GB before bodies, replicas and indexes. Result, journal and provider dedup retention differ; TTL expiry does not establish external absence. Track queue age and drain rates: a million events with 500/second spare throughput take at least 2,000 seconds to drain under steady arrivals.

5. Trace an operation across authority boundaries

The submit trace separates local atomic intent from external execution. A crash after processing but before local commit requires retained downstream identity and outcome resolution rather than a new payment. Leases/epochs prevent stale local writes only when receivers enforce them; they do not cancel in-flight external requests.

Record traces distinguish authorization from financial events, while reconciliation exposes completeness obligations. Diagrams show logical dependencies and asynchronous intent without claiming one transaction covers processors, merchant systems or banks. A separate journal service needs committed intents, idempotent posting and explicit consistency/availability policies, not naive dual writes.

7. Crash-safe idempotency is more than a cache key

Four durable checkpoints

  1. Claim

    Validate principal/fingerprint and uniquely claim the operation.

  2. Record

    Persist attempts/outbox, owners and routes before sending.

  3. Resolve

    Preserve external identity, uncertainty and authoritative evidence.

  4. Commit / replay

    Commit results and intents; replay preserves declared semantics.

Each checkpoint needs crash/retry tests and retained identity.

Contract example: Stripe API v1

  • Once endpoint execution starts, keys retain the first status/body, including 500s; validation or pre-execution conflicts do not retain a result.

Contract example: Stripe API v2

  • Retries do not repeat successful operations; failed/partial work may continue and return an updated response.

A unique claim before side effects is necessary, but a crash immediately after a standalone claim can leave an orphan. Atomically create claims, immutable fingerprints, operations/attempts and dispatch intent. Recoverable workers use ownership/epochs, inspect state and preserve external keys. After provider dedup expiry, use evidence, reconciliation or controlled resolution rather than blind resubmission.

Commit result transitions coherently with financial/event intents. Shared-store journals can use local transactions; separate stores need replay-safe protocols and explicit accepted/posted visibility. Link replay metadata to operations rather than completing a cache before financial state. Two identical purchases remain separate intents, not amount/card/time duplicates.

Idempotency does not mean every API replays identical bytes for every error. Pin the API family/version and its retention, validation, concurrent-work and result contract. This chapter’s endpoints declare their own semantics; do not transfer v1 assumptions to v2 or another provider.

8. Do not turn stale status into reroute proof

Compare three histories: A authorized but lost its reply; A never executes and later gives terminal fenced-absence proof; or A queues and executes late. All show stale NOT_FOUND at step three. Rerouting on that observation can create duplicate holds. Breakers guide new work under ownership rules rather than rewriting pending outcomes.

A connection refusal is safe evidence only when the attempt was never submitted anywhere and has no earlier retries or in-flight workers. Proxies and hidden retries can break that inference. Status needs consistency and terminality; current absence does not prevent future execution. Without provider-supported cancellation/fencing proof, preserve uncertainty, inform merchants and resolve through bounded escalation/reconciliation.

9. Webhooks: authenticity, durable inbox and business effects

Receivers verify raw bytes, signatures and delivery timestamps using the intended secret. Parsing/reserializing can change bytes. Time tolerance, clock sync, rotation and account context are separate checks. Valid signatures establish authenticity under a contract, not novelty, ordering or current state. Retries may refresh delivery timestamps; event creation times are not duplicate identities.

After verification, durably admit a unique inbox record and ACK without waiting for shipping. Workers guard domain transitions and atomically record fulfillment intent; external delivery uses stable sink identities. Inbox dedup blocks repeated event IDs, while different events for one payment need business guards. Late processing cannot regress success. Refunds/disputes remain separate legitimate workflows; success is not permission to ignore all future events.

10. Balanced does not establish correctness or completeness

For a 5000-minor collection, illustrative entries debit processor receivables 5000 and credit merchant payable 4850 plus fee revenue 150. Settlement debits cash and credits receivables under actual evidence/fee policy; payouts reduce payables against cash or clearing under their workflows. Account charts and recognition depend on business models; this illustrates balancing rather than prescribing accounting policy.

Duplicating or omitting a whole balanced transaction can preserve balance while misrepresenting liabilities. Enforce unique economic events, amount/currency checks, completeness/frontiers and external reconciliation. Materialized balances are valid with atomic/versioned maintenance and journal verification; a balance column is not inherently unsafe. Conservation also misses wrong-account postings.

Import reports with versions, line deduplication, covered intervals and completion state. Match identities, amounts, currencies, dates and fees; totals can hide offsetting errors. Clearing balances may be normal in-flight state, so use aging/maturity expectations. Approved reversals/adjustments retain references instead of erasing audit history.

11. Availability, reserves and bank returns

Explain a payout through timeouts and returns

  1. Check eligibility

    Check available funds, reserves, restrictions and currency; authorization is not cash.

  2. Reserve and submit

    Reserve atomically with a durable instruction; retries retain identity.

  3. Resolve evidence

    Timeouts remain pending/unknown; evidence governs transitions rather than blind resend.

  4. Account and reconcile

    Confirmed movement or later returns create referenced postings/cases while preserving history.

This generic workflow follows rail contracts; steps require their own identities and evidence.

Availability is not simply the sum of successful payments. Settlement timing, reserves, refunds, disputes, currencies and restrictions determine eligibility. Atomically reserve payouts against concurrent refunds/transfers, without spending again on instruction retries. Banks may return sent/paid instructions later; model and reconcile returns under rail contracts.

Define fee, seller, refund/dispute and negative-balance responsibilities and permissions. FX needs quote expiry, rate sources, rounding and balanced per-currency postings through conversion accounts; adding USD to EUR is not balancing. Cross-cell and external workflows need references and recovery so in-flight funds remain explainable.

12. Risk and tokenization are distinct boundaries

Risk outcome

  • Allow, challenge, review or deny under owned, versioned evidence and timeout policies.

Card-data boundary

  • Hosted collection, secure vaults and scoped tokens without sensitive-field logging.

Operational proof

  • Test latency, token misuse, rotation, replay and access logging.

Inline risk needs bounded features and deadlines, evaluated by approvals, loss, false declines, challenge conversion, freshness and latency by cohort. Delayed labels and approved-only samples create bias. Timeout fallback belongs to owned business/security policy rather than one universal rule. Issuer declines follow method/network policy, not attempts across every processor to evade hard declines.

Hosted collection reduces app exposure by keeping fields within provider boundaries. Tokens can still confer capabilities or link identities, requiring scope, permissions and redacted logs. Test vault isolation, keys, rotation, auditing and controlled detokenization. PAN isolation alone does not establish every other system’s scope; scripts, logs, support access and integration choices still matter.

13. Migrate schemas and cells while writes continue

Expand schemas/stores, start reliable change capture, backfill source versions, compare reads and cut over after verified frontiers. A snapshot may be read before live updates but applied after CDC; blind upserts overwrite new versions. Use monotonic versions/tombstones and equal-version conflict handling. Equality needs matching payload/schema semantics; retained deletion evidence prevents resurrection.

Readiness includes change barriers, completed backfill, validation, dependencies and sink fencing, not equal row counts. Rolling back after new-only writes requires reverse sync or freeze/repair, not a routing flip. Every write sink must activate authority epochs; a token unknown to the sink cannot fence earlier work. The fixture explicitly activates authority at handoff.

14. Public-contract versions differ from data versions

API versions define agreed client shapes and semantics. Schemas describe stored state; object versions/epochs govern concurrency and ownership. These are not interchangeable. Response transformations help shape changes, while side effects, permissions and state semantics require behavior logic and tests. Decode pinned webhook formats correctly when replaying old events.

Compatibility has costs. Maintain contract fixtures, opt-in canaries, deprecation windows and rollback ownership. URLs, headers or account pinning can work under explicit lifecycles. Distinguish SDK versions from API contracts. Merchants may prefer hosted checkout; this chapter teaches platform correctness rather than recommending every merchant build orchestration.

15. Cells and admission protect accepted work

Home-cell routing uses authenticated tenants and epochs, never sending one in-flight operation to two owners after timeouts. Cells help when shared control planes, identity/config and rollout are controlled. Handoffs drain/freeze, replicate operations/results/idempotency, verify destinations and fence sources. Copying payment rows without dedup, attempts and outboxes can duplicate work after failover.

Placement requirements depend on jurisdiction/integration; topology does not certify compliance. Include replicas, backups, logs, traces, analytics, support access and key policies. This is not jurisdiction-specific legal advice. Cell/cohort rollout can bound impact, while unguarded shared configuration can still cause global failure.

Use fairness and bounded admission queues, defer exports, reserve recovery/status capacity and reject excess work promptly. Accepted work retains durable ownership and escalation. Breakers route new work only within backup capacity with bounded probes/ramps. Duplicate-safe retries still cost authentication, lookups, CPU and storage; bound attempts/deadlines and jitter under documented contracts.

17. Practice with concrete faults

Follow one intent through external evidence and merchant fulfillment. When constraints change, explain authority, retained state and failure windows before adding components. Answers use this chapter’s contracts rather than assuming all providers support identical status/cancellation semantics.

Is claiming an idempotency key before charging sufficient?

No. Atomically link claims to attempts and dispatch intents with recovery ownership/results. Preserve downstream identity under provider scope/window contracts; resolve crashes after effects rather than inventing attempts. API replay does not cover processors or fulfillment sinks.

Can you route to B after a timeout and NOT_FOUND?

Only with authoritative terminal absence and prevention of old execution. Replicas and queues can report temporary absence. Without proof, preserve uncertainty and reconcile. The same ID at B does not imply cross-provider deduplication.

Can approved authorization be recorded as settled cash?

No. Authorization is typically a hold/approval; capture, settlement and payout have separate evidence/timing. Accounting may represent commitments/pending items under policy but cannot invent final bank movement from approval or HTTP 202.

Why can event-ID dedup still ship twice?

Different events can report one successful payment. Inbox dedup protects delivery identity; guarded payment transitions and unique fulfillment intents protect business effects. External shipping needs stable sink identity/recovery, and late processing cannot regress success.

Does a balanced ledger prove all money is correct?

No. Duplicated/missing balanced transactions and wrong merchants can still balance. Check identities, amounts, currencies/accounts, completeness and external reconciliation. Evaluate clearing maturity rather than forcing zero without evidence.

Does tokenization make all non-vault services harmless?

No. Tokens may enable payments or link people; permissions, scripts, logs and support access need review. Hosted collection reduces exposure without independently certifying scope/compliance or removing merchant responsibilities.

Can you switch reads once dual writing and backfill finish?

Verify frontiers, payloads, versions, tombstones, consistency and active fences. Late v1 cannot overwrite CDCv2. Rolling back new-only writes needs reverse sync or repair; equal row counts are insufficient.

Does prioritizing payments prohibit rejecting any payment?

No. Bound admission before acceptance, then retain accepted-work ownership and recovery. Defer reports without indefinitely starving status/reconciliation. Idempotent retries and backup providers still consume finite resources.
