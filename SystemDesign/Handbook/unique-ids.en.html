Unique IDs: Bit Layouts, Clocks and Index Locality

Design uniqueness across restarts and nodes; explore Snowflake bits, B-tree locality and ID precision across APIs.

1. Uniqueness, ordering and secrecy are different requirements

Identify within the right scope

  • Unique across restarts, failover and data merging.
  • Does the key include a namespace/tenant?

Promised ordering

  • Per-generator, approximate-time or global-sequence order?
  • Distinguish ID time, event time and commit order.

Information exposure

  • Counters and timestamps can reveal volume or timing.
  • Opaque identifiers still need server-side authorization.

Define uniqueness across tables, tenants, clusters, restarts and migrations. Then establish size, ordering, peak IDs/ms, offline operation and guessability. Unique IDs do not replace authorization: knowing or guessing a URL must not grant resource access.

Strict global order, approximate time order and uniqueness have different costs. Clock skew can make local generators emit apparently future IDs; timestamp prefixes do not prove causality. Gap-free invoice numbers are a separate business requirement, often needing transactional allocation rather than object IDs that happen not to skip.

2. Compare the option space

Prioritize storage and consumer needs. A 128-bit scheme without worker allocation may cost less than operating a 64-bit service. Conversely, primary keys repeated in secondary indexes can make size and locality significant for particular workloads.

3. Snowflake: allocate a 63-bit budget

A common layout keeps the sign bit zero and uses 41 relative-millisecond bits, 10 worker bits and 12 sequence bits. It represents 1,024 workers, 4,096 sequence values per millisecond per worker and about 69.7 years. These are representational limits, not throughput benchmarks or distributed guarantees.

ID = (relativeTime << (workerBits+sequenceBits)) | (worker << sequenceBits) | sequence. Validate field ranges rather than masking overflow and continuing. Sequence exhaustion may require waiting or a proven logical-time policy with explicit latency and drift bounds.

The fixture clock is a manually controlled relative-millisecond count, not wall time. Issuing increments the sequence at one timestamp; an over-capacity burst accepts a valid prefix and rejects the remainder. Clock rollback retains the watermark and refuses issuance; advancing past it starts a new timestamp and sequence. At most six samples represent even large batches without creating hundreds of thousands of UI rows.

4. Generator state must survive concurrency and restarts

Checks before issuing an ID

  1. Validate authority

    No duplicate worker/epoch allocation.

  2. Read and validate time

    Do not reenter an issued time range.

  3. Allocate the sequence

    Atomic; no wrap under one timestamp.

  4. Encode and return a string

    Validate fields and preserve API precision.

This is a decision sequence; failed checks must wait, safely change authority or return an error rather than silently reuse fields.

Timestamp/sequence updates need appropriate serialization or atomicity within a process. Concurrent threads can issue duplicates despite a valid worker ID. Resetting sequence after restart under a reused timestamp/worker can reissue a range; shutdown-only persistence does not survive crashes or power loss.

Options include a durable high watermark, a new epoch/worker namespace, or waiting until previously issued ranges cannot recur under the protocol. Each costs latency, availability or horizon. A fixed startup sleep proves nothing without clock/reuse bounds; plan epoch rollover before time bits run out.

The lab keeps memory-only state and has no worker allocator. It explains one worker’s clock/sequence without proving crash or shared-worker safety. Resetting or changing layouts starts an independent experiment; doing so in production under the same namespace/time can duplicate IDs. Reserve durable watermarks/ranges or non-overlapping namespaces before exposing identifiers.

5. Worker leases do not automatically stop stale generators

Worker identity must be unique within its namespace and reuse horizon. Stateful ordinals alone are insufficient: two clusters can share an ordinal, or an old instance may outlive its replacement. Expiring a lease does not terminate a paused process.

Reissuing the same worker without an encoded/enforced fencing epoch may let a resumed stale generator collide. Prevent overlapping ranges/time, enforce fencing at authority use, or encode/validate epochs. Stopping when lease loss is detected is insufficient while a paused/partitioned process has not detected it.

6. Clock skew differs from clock rollback

Skew between distinct workers primarily disturbs time ordering, not tuple uniqueness. Rollback within one generator can reuse tuples without protective state. NTP slew/step behavior depends on configuration; wall-clock monotonicity cannot be assumed.

Refusing or waiting preserves safety at an availability cost. Logical timestamps can continue in some designs but diverge from wall time and still exhaust sequence space. Observe regressions, drift, sequence saturation and worker conflicts. Clock synchronization helps but does not replace a uniqueness protocol.

7. UUIDv4, UUIDv7 and ULID

UUIDv4 has 122 random bits after version/variant. For n independent uniform samples, small collision probability is approximately n(n−1)/(2×2^122). It is not impossibility: broken generators, weak randomness or cloned state can dominate the birthday calculation. Storage uniqueness constraints remain useful.

UUIDv7 is 128 bits with a 48-bit Unix-millisecond prefix plus version/variant fields; remaining bits may combine randomness and implementation-specific counter/submillisecond constructs. Entropy and monotonic behavior vary. ULID is also time-prefixed but has different encoding/specification and is not interchangeable with UUIDv7.

Within one millisecond, v7 sort order may differ from invocation order. Per-generator monotonicity is not a system-wide strict order. Use a tested implementation and verify wire format and storage ordering rather than labeling an arbitrary timestamp/random concatenation UUIDv7.

8. Index locality: separate key order from engine behavior

Random inserts may touch many leaves; time-prefixed keys often improve locality without guaranteeing every insert appends to one leaf. Splits, fill factors, concurrency, caching and distribution determine performance. A toy tree cannot establish an N-times speedup.

InnoDB clusters rows by primary key and stores primary-key values in secondary indexes, so key width affects several structures. PostgreSQL B-trees do not automatically cluster table storage like InnoDB; heap layout and index locality differ. Avoid declaring UUIDv4 universally bad for every database.

9. 64-bit IDs through JavaScript and JSON

JavaScript Number exactly represents every integer only through 2^53−1. Signed 64-bit IDs can exceed it; parsing JSON numbers may round them before validation. Use decimal strings throughout APIs, clients, logs, CSVs and cache keys.

BigInt handles 64-bit arithmetic but is not directly JSON-stringifiable by default. Convert deliberately to strings at boundaries and use suitable numeric comparison or contractually fixed-width representations. Lexicographic order of “10” and “2” is not numeric order.

const id = 9007199254740993n;
const body = JSON.stringify({ id: id.toString() });
// {"id":"9007199254740993"}
// Keep the ID as a string in clients; parse as BigInt only for numeric operations.

This preserves the integer; its JSON-number counterpart is unsafe for Number-based clients.

10. Good locality can coexist with hot partitions

Range-sharding directly on increasing IDs concentrates new writes in the newest range. Time ordering within a tenant partition may help, but ownership needs its own tenant/hash/workload design. Hash prefixes distribute writes at a cost to range queries and sorting.

Public timestamp/counter IDs can reveal information. Separate internal keys from public opaque IDs when justified, accounting for uniqueness, lookups and migration. Random-looking identifiers are not automatically suitable reset tokens or bearer secrets, which need their own entropy, expiry and access policies.

11. Generator migration and operations

Define namespaces/versions preventing old/new overlap when changing schemes. Expand schema, use bounded dual-read mappings, throttle backfills, validate FK/cache/event consumers and then switch writes. Public IDs often need permanent resolution; a column change does not update every downstream string/128-bit consumer.

Observe uniqueness violations, allocation latency, worker reuse, clock regressions and remaining horizon. Test ungraceful crashes, rapid restarts, concurrent generation, lease loss and cross-environment dataset merging. Validate collision prevention against the failure model, not just generation throughput.

12. Interview drill: test the difficult edges

What fits a million IDs/s with 64-bit and approximate-time ordering?

Snowflake-style is a candidate, subject to per-worker peak/ms, namespaces, clock/restart policy and reuse safety. 41/10/12 provides representational ceilings. Add headroom, benchmark and serialize strings. If 128 bits are allowed, compare UUIDv7’s operating cost before introducing a service.

Evenly distributing 1,000,000 IDs/s across four workers averages 250 IDs/ms/worker, while twelve sequence bits represent 4,096 values/ms. Averages do not size the fleet: a burst of 6,000 requests to one worker in one millisecond exceeds sequence space despite an acceptable fleet average. Measure per-millisecond peaks, skew, contention and wait latency; define backpressure or rejection semantics.

Then choose the custom epoch and remaining horizon. Growing worker bits from ten to thirteen while retaining twelve sequence bits shrinks time from forty-one to thirty-eight bits, reducing the horizon from about 69.68 to 8.71 years. A past epoch consumes part of that horizon. Plan rollover/versioning and consumer compatibility before exhausting the range.

A library or service can fit different ownership, languages and operating needs. A library removes a per-ID network hop but not worker allocation, fencing or crash-state problems. Validate fields rather than mask overflow, serialize decimal strings and test paused/restarted workers. If 64 bits are negotiable, compare UUIDv7 under actual ordering, RNG and storage requirements without promising every insertion appends.

Does worker-lease expiry absolutely prevent duplicate IDs?

Not if a stale paused holder resumes after the same worker is reissued. Prevent namespace/time overlap or use appropriate epochs/fencing; self-stopping on detected lease loss still depends on detection.

Suppose A issues (t=100, worker=7, sequence=0), pauses, and worker 7 is leased to B. Reusing that tuple produces the identical packed value; an external lease identity does not change the formula. Fencing one sink can reject stale writes without making IDs already exposed to caches/logs/clients globally unique. Define issuance versus acceptance guarantees and protect every relevant boundary; any required epoch/namespace must be encoded or accompany identity everywhere.

Can time-ordered IDs replace commit order?

No. Allocation may precede a transaction that retries or rolls back, and clocks may differ. Exact ordering needs an appropriate log sequence or commit-metadata contract.

Is UUIDv7 always faster than UUIDv4?

No. It often improves locality, but engines, caches, distribution, concurrency and width matter. Both are 128 bits; benchmark the workload and distinguish InnoDB clustering from PostgreSQL heap/index behavior.

Why might an ID change after passing through the frontend?

Check for a hop parsing 64-bit IDs into Number. Preserve strings at every boundary, test values above 2^53−1 and inspect analytics/CSV/cache paths as well as the main endpoint.

Should gap-free invoice numbering use Snowflake?

Do not equate object identifiers with business numbering. Define issuance, cancellation, auditing and transactional authority. Snowflake, cached sequences and leased ranges may leave gaps; retrying to fill numbers is not the solution.

Open questions: explain the underlying mechanisms

Attempt an answer before reading, then change an assumption and reconsider the conclusion. Answers retain units and failure boundaries; the preceding sections provide diagrams and models.

How can a ticket allocator fail over without reissuing identifiers?

A central counter or range allocator can be simple, but issued ranges need durable progress. Consensus-backed allocation or atomic database reservations require explicit commit, failover and split-brain semantics. A new leader reading stale allocation state can reissue identifiers; asynchronous replica promotion does not inherently preserve uniqueness.

Leased blocks reduce coordination while introducing gaps on losses or restarts; do not reuse uncertain ranges. Fence old allocators and test paused leaders. Legally controlled gap-free numbering differs from technical uniqueness and requires an audited issuance workflow. Offline block allocation alone cannot promise both unconstrained availability and gap-free issuance.
