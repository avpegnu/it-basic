Replication, Consistency and Consensus

Follow data across replicas, test read guarantees, understand quorums and Raft, and choose consistency from invariants rather than database names.

Many copies, one testable promise

  1. Choose write authority

    Single leader, multi-leader or leaderless.

  2. State the read guarantee

    Freshness, sessions, order and transactions.

  3. Test distributed behavior

    Lag, quorums, partitions and failover.

  4. Check the invariant

    Which failures are tolerable, and which require refusing work?

Read from the client’s perspective: where was the write accepted, where may reads occur, and what happens when groups of nodes lose contact?

  • Replication creates copies; consistency specifies what clients may observe.
  • An acknowledgement needs a durable-state and replica-set contract, not just the word “synchronous”.
  • R + W > N ensures intersection for fixed replica sets; it does not by itself establish linearizability.
  • An old leader can remain alive without a quorum to commit new writes.
  • Leases need protection against a resumed stale holder; convergence does not preserve every invariant.

1. Who may accept a write?

Single-leader · One write owner

  • Clients send writes to a leader; replicas receive its log/state.
  • Failover must select suitable data and fence the old owner.

Multi-leader · Multiple writers

  • Writes may enter multiple authorized regions or nodes.
  • Concurrent updates may require merging, rejection or per-key ownership.

Leaderless · Coordinate replicas

  • A coordinator reads/writes a replica set under a chosen policy.
  • Repair, version resolution and membership all affect the guarantee.

Replication supports durability, continued operation after node failure and read scaling. It also creates state to synchronize: a write may be committed in one place but not applied elsewhere. Before adding replicas, define the replication unit: database, shard or partition.

A leader can order writes for a partition, while applications still need isolation, constraints and idempotency. A single leader does not automatically prevent write skew, double booking or duplicate payments on retry. Multiple leaders require conflict detection and resolution before promising active-active operation.

2. How safe is a write when it is acknowledged?

Separate four milestones: memory receipt, log writing, flushing under the durability contract, and application to query-visible state. Receipt is not durability, and a durable replica log need not already be query-visible. This matters for both failover and read-your-writes.

Asynchronous replication may acknowledge before a replica receives the write, so promoting a lagging replica can lose an acknowledged write. Waiting for replicas or a quorum reduces risk within the stated failure model, not against every loss scenario. Specify flush locations, eligible promotion candidates and simultaneous failure domains.

Example acknowledgement policyWhat does the client know?Remaining question
Local durableThe owner holds a durable record under its configuration.Does the promotion candidate contain this record if the owner is lost?
Remote receipt / writeA replica set has received or written the record.Was it flushed or only written to an OS buffer? Is it query-visible?
Remote applyReplicas have applied the record under the configured contract.Will the next read actually reach a replica meeting that watermark?

3. What visible anomalies does lag create?

Read-your-writes lets a session observe its completed writes. Monotonic reads prevent a session from regressing behind an earlier observation. They differ: after reading version 2 and writing version 3, reading version 2 is monotonic but violates read-your-writes.

One option is reading from the write owner; another carries a session watermark, such as a log position, and only reads replicas applied through it. Otherwise wait with a deadline or follow a fallback policy. Routing to the primary for a few seconds is heuristic without a lag bound. Replica pinning helps, but failover or state regression still requires version checks.

A consistent prefix concerns dependency order: an answer should not appear without its prerequisite question within the guarantee’s scope. Across partitions, one offset from partition A does not describe progress in partition B. A session token may need multiple watermarks or explicit dependency tracking.

4. Consistency and isolation are not the same axis

Name the operation and object scope before saying “strong.” Linearizability respects the real-time order of nonoverlapping operations within its guaranteed object scope. Serializability makes transactions equivalent to a serial execution without necessarily preserving real-time order. Strict serializability adds real-time order to the transaction guarantee.

Do not place every guarantee on one line and infer all implication relationships. Causal, session and transaction guarantees describe different aspects; an API can combine them. Eventual convergence needs propagation/merge conditions; updates lost forever or stalled repair do not converge merely because a store is called eventual.

GuaranteeWhat it constrainsWhat it does not imply

5. During a partition: what returns, waits or is refused?

When communication breaks

  • Protect the invariant by waiting or refusing writes without authority.
  • Or continue operations whose semantics tolerate divergence and repair.

When communication works

  • Place quorums/owners around latency and failure domains.
  • Measure replication coordination instead of assuming one RTT for every region.

In CAP, consistency means atomic/linearizable behavior, while availability requires requests to nonfailed nodes eventually receive suitable responses rather than being refused to preserve consistency. This differs from an operational SLO’s percentage of good requests. A network partition prevents simultaneously guaranteeing both properties for every execution in the model.

Answer per operation: without quorum, booking confirmation may become unavailable rather than confirming uncertain inventory. Search may return older results under its stated semantics. Changing an HTTP status does not turn a refused operation into a successful available execution. PACELC adds the normal-operation question: how much replication coordination and latency does the chosen consistency require?

6. Quorums: prove the intersection condition

For one fixed home set of N replicas, a write waits for W acknowledgements and a read for R responses. Their minimum intersection is max(0, R + W − N). With N=5, W=3, R=3 it is at least one node. If F nodes fail and all remaining nodes are reachable, reads/writes require N−F ≥ R and N−F ≥ W respectively.

This is a set-intersection proof, not proof of a linearizable register. Version semantics, concurrent writes, repair/write-back, failed writes and durability also matter. A timed-out write may have modified some replicas and become visible to a later read. Picking the largest timestamp does not turn clock skew into correct real-time order.

A sloppy quorum may accept acknowledgements from nodes outside the home set during failures. The fixed-N intersection proof cannot then ignore substitute nodes. Hinted handoff and anti-entropy aid repair, but require stated convergence and repair conditions rather than calling every quorum level “strong.”

7. Raft: distinguish term, log and commit

A term is a logical epoch. A node votes at most once per term; a majority elects a candidate whose log is sufficiently up to date. Majority intersection prevents two leaders in one term. A partitioned old leader may not know of a newer term, but cannot commit a conflicting history.

Raft advances commit using a current-term entry replicated to a majority; earlier entries commit through the prefix. Replicas apply committed entries in order. Membership changes, persistence and snapshots have additional rules. The selected frames distinguish having a leader, containing an entry and having committed it.

A node believing it is leader does not establish a linearizable read. The implementation must validate authority and apply through the required position; quorum/read-index barriers and lease-based reads have distinct conditions. Lease reads depend on timing assumptions, so a database’s use of Raft does not replace its API read contract.

8. Where should consensus run, and what does it cost?

Safety · What must not go wrong

  • Committed history must not be replaced by a conflicting one.
  • Increasing timeouts is not a safety proof.

Liveness · When progress is possible

  • Enough nodes and suitable communication/timing must permit election and replication.
  • A system preserving safety may temporarily refuse writes.

Consensus commonly manages membership, configuration and ownership in the control plane. Replicated databases also legitimately use it on the data path, often per shard/partition, when data needs those ordering and safety guarantees. Neither routing every user write through one metadata cluster nor forbidding data-plane consensus is a universal rule.

A majority of N nodes is floor(N/2)+1. Three and five nodes tolerate at most one and two unavailable nodes respectively, provided the required nodes communicate and liveness conditions hold; six still tolerate only two by this calculation. Failure-domain placement matters: five nodes in one failed power domain do not survive that domain’s loss.

9. Clocks, versions and merging concurrent updates

Wall clocks may skew or move backwards; a larger timestamp does not prove causal succession. Last-write-wins discards one side under a chosen order and may suit data where that loss is acceptable, but does not preserve arbitrary increments/decrements. Lamport clocks respect causal order; distinct logical timestamps alone do not establish causality.

A version vector tracks counters per actor/replica. Neither {A:2,B:1} nor {A:1,B:2} dominates the other, so they are concurrent. {A:3,B:2} dominates both and can represent a version observing/merging them under the protocol. Large or changing actor sets increase metadata and require semantically safe pruning.

State-based CRDTs combine associative, commutative and idempotent merge with suitable state/update conditions; operation-based CRDTs have type-specific delivery/order requirements. Convergence does not automatically preserve invariants such as nonnegative stock when two regions sell the final unit. Use suitable ownership, escrow or coordination when free merging violates the invariant.

10. An expired lease does not make the old worker disappear

Enforce fencing where data changes

  1. Grant a new epoch

    B receives token 42.

  2. The resource observes it

    A token-42 write/registration is accepted.

  3. Fence the stale holder

    A sends token 41; the resource rejects it.

After token 42 has been recorded, a late token-41 write must be rejected by an atomic resource-side check.

Worker A pauses while holding a lease. The lease expires and B acquires new authority, but A can resume and send a write. Having acquired a lock earlier does not protect that side effect. A random ownership token helps release the right lease; an increasing fencing token distinguishes old and new authority at the resource.

The resource must atomically validate and record the token with the write. After accepting token 42, it rejects token 41. Issuing tokens without enforcement at the side-effect boundary provides no fencing. Writes arriving before the resource learns the new epoch need defined semantics; fencing does not mean two processes never execute simultaneously.

11. 2PC, sagas and replication solve different problems

Atomic commit across participants

  • Requires protocol support and recovery from all participating resources.
  • Explain locks, prepared state, timeouts and unknown outcomes.

Local commits and compensation

  • Intermediate states may be visible.
  • Compensations need ownership, retries and manual resolution paths where necessary.

Replicating a state machine does not make a transaction across independent owners atomic. Two-phase commit coordinates participants’ commit/abort decision: prepare records readiness, then participants follow the durable decision. A participant unable to recover an unknown decision may block. Replicating the coordinator improves recovery but does not remove every participant dependency.

A saga uses local transactions with compensations: for example, reserve stock → charge → create shipment. A refund is a new transaction, not erasure of the original charge. It needs state machines, idempotency, timeouts, retries and failed-compensation handling. Choose by resources, invariants and tolerated intermediate states, not a slogan that microservices must be eventual.

12. Choose guarantees per operation and test failures

Write an operation contract before choosing a product: invariants, permitted staleness, session-token propagation and behavior without authority. A profile page may need read-your-writes while like counts converge eventually. Updating balances across accounts needs an explicit transaction boundary, not merely a per-key linearizability label.

Observe apply lag, watermark waits, owner fallbacks, election frequency, quorum latency and repair backlog. Inject network delay/drop, process pauses, post-acknowledgement leader crashes, lagging-replica failover and retries after unknown outcomes. Record operation histories and validate invariants; green dashboards and replica counts do not prove consistency.

13. Explain the guarantees through failure scenarios

Do N=3, R=2, W=2 establish linearizability?

No. They prove an intersection of at least one node for the same fixed home set. Version resolution, concurrent/partial writes, the read protocol and durability still matter. Sloppy quorums or timestamp conflict resolution under clock skew can invalidate a latest-write claim.

If a write times out after one replica receives it, may a later read observe it?

Why can a user see the old name immediately after editing it?

The owner may have completed the write while the next read reaches an unapplied replica. Carry the write position in the session and require the watermark, or read the owner. If the deadline prevents waiting, define fallback/error behavior rather than silently weakening the promise.

Do two nodes believing they are leaders imply a Raft safety failure?

Not necessarily: a partition can leave leaders in different terms. Examine quorum authority and actual commits. An old leader without quorum cannot commit conflicting history and steps down on observing a higher term. External side-effect resources may also require fencing.

Does a 30-second lease guarantee that only one worker writes a file?

No. A holder can pause beyond the lease and resume. The resource needs suitable fencing or conditional updates; checking a lock at function entry cannot stop a late write. If enforcement is unavailable, redesign the workflow or explicitly accept the risk rather than claiming absolute mutual exclusion.

Does CRDT convergence preserve nonnegative stock?

Not automatically. Two regions seeing stock=1 may both sell one unit and merge to −1 under the chosen data type. Convergence means agreeing on a result; an invariant restricts which results are allowed. Choose operations, escrow, ownership or coordination that actually preserves it.

What should you check after failover loses an acknowledged write?

Check the acknowledgement contract, the promoted replica’s log/flush/apply positions, membership and fencing of the old owner. Asynchronous replication or an unsuitable promotion candidate may explain it, alongside durability configuration and failure domains. Reconcile caches, events and downstream effects that observed the lost write.
