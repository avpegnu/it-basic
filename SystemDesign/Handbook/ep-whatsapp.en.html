1. Private messengers: relays, archives and endpoint trust

Delivery

  • Acceptance, device durability and reading have distinct guarantees.

Content trust

  • Approved endpoint identities/protocols protect content while metadata has separate controls.

Recovery

  • Queues, history synchronization and backup material have different lifecycles.

This lesson builds a WhatsApp-style private person-to-person messenger with direct/group messages, offline devices, attachments, companions and backups. Choosing store-and-forward queues instead of a complete server history archive is a design policy, not a requirement of E2EE: servers can retain encrypted history under different retention/authorization contracts. The 12 stages are a teaching architecture rather than an inventory of a current production system.

Approved endpoints can read, share or report content. Compromised endpoints and user-saved copies lie outside a promise that intermediaries relay only ciphertext. Business integrations, bots/AI and reporting must declare their own plaintext endpoint/trust boundaries rather than inherit assumptions from private human chat. Worksheets generate no keys, encrypt nothing and do not implement Signal; they examine counts, histories and assumed key possession.

2. One message has multiple identities and acknowledgements

Accept → persist → deliver → observe

  1. Prepare

    Audited protocol and stable envelopes.

  2. Accept

    Durable obligations under retention/RPO.

  3. Persist

    Recipient persistence/dedup before acknowledgement.

  4. Observe

    Delivery differs from explicit reading.

Transitions require identity and recovery rather than successful socket writes.

Retain logical message IDs bound to senders/conversations, immutable envelope identities for recipient devices/generations, membership epochs and accepted-result records. Retrying prepared ciphertext preserves its envelope; legitimate re-encryption uses a new revision while retaining logical intent where the protocol permits. Do not invent ratchets/state machines from a diagram: outgoing protocol state and persisted envelopes need audited implementation/recovery to avoid key reuse or lost state.

A server acknowledgment means the selected durability boundary accepted an obligation. A device acknowledgment means the selected local persistence boundary accepted it. Reading is a separate explicit action/policy. Ciphertext may be durably stored awaiting keys/decryption under a declared contract, but this is not user reading. Server garbage collection completes only exact authenticated device obligations or explicitly records retention expiry.

4. Size sockets, envelopes, queues and media separately

The hypothetical fixture uses 20M concurrent devices with 16 KiB application state/device; 30M DAU sending 40 logical messages/day with a mean of 3 recipient-device deliveries/message; 10M offline devices with 6 queued messages of 2 KB each; and 5% of messages carrying 1 MB media. These are not WhatsApp metrics and exclude kernel/TLS state, indexes, replicas and headroom.

Concurrent sockets set memory, file-descriptor and heartbeat demand. Encryption/envelope fan-out determines client CPU/data and relay work; media bytes have independent caching/retention. Measure node capacity with actual workloads, security and runtime settings. Reconnect surges can exceed ordinary sending load; recovery state and key/device directories must sustain that demand too.

5. Direct delivery, receipts, groups and restore

Edges describe responsibilities/trust boundaries, not real cryptographic APIs. The key directory stores public bundles/approval evidence, but clients must verify relevant identities. Backup vaults may retain protected secret material through separate protocols; do not claim every backend contains only public keys under every recovery mode.

Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

          7. Durable device receipts and local deduplication

          The lab sends one message to D1/D2: acknowledging D1 cannot delete offline D2 obligations. If D1 acknowledges volatile-only data and then crashes, garbage collection leaves no replay path. If D2 persists but its acknowledgment is lost, redelivery under the same logical identity must not add a duplicate local message. Recipient state/results/receipts need coherent crash recovery; a unique ID alone does not serialize effects.

          Per-device queues retain accepted ciphertext under retention, storage durability and authentication/epoch contracts. Devices missing keys/context may durably retain pending-decryption items and request protocol repair; undecrypted content is not read. Queue TTL expiry needs an expired/failed outcome. Push notifications are wake hints, not durable delivery.

          8. Approved identities, metadata and endpoint threat models

          Protect content

          • Use audited protocols with approved identities and context.

          Bound metadata

          • Minimize and govern routing/log data.

          State exclusions

          • Saved plaintext, endpoint compromise and authorized reporting remain explicit boundaries.

          E2EE differs from TLS: securing the wire to a service does not prevent that endpoint’s operator from reading content. Approved endpoint-key ownership, authenticated session setup, per-message evolution, bounded replay/out-of-order handling and device verification jointly define the contract. A malicious directory may introduce a device; approval, consistency and security-code verification need concrete semantics. Fetching a public key is not proof of trust.

          Depending on the design, routing, device/account/group membership, times, sizes and media access remain metadata. Encryption does not itself provide anonymity or prevent authorized recipients from sharing/reporting or endpoint malware from reading plaintext. Cryptographic forward secrecy under erased-key assumptions does not delete plaintext history saved on devices. Declare authorized plaintext endpoints for private communications, integrations and backups.

          9. Sender Keys optimize senders, not recipient delivery

          Senders distribute chain state and public signing-verification keys over approved pairwise sessions; private signing keys are not shared with the group. Subsequent payloads use protocol message-key evolution/erasure and signing before relay fan-out. Member departures require clearing/starting fresh state under the chosen scheme; inactive senders may defer distribution until their next send. Churn/device changes need per-sender/per-group generations.

          The cost lab retains A surviving active senders, each sending M messages per epoch, with only non-senders leaving. At epoch k, recipient devices are Rk=(N−k)×D−1, including the sender’s companions but excluding the originating device. Pairwise data envelopes=A×M×ΣRk; Sender Key distribution=A×ΣRk; shared payloads=A×M×epochs. Both schemes retain A×M×ΣRk server data deliveries. These are logical counts, not equal-cost cryptographic benchmarks.

          Short/churny conversations may not amortize setup. Packet batching, ciphertext sizes, key/session establishment, signatures, retries and actual CPU/battery use need separate measurement. Other group-key-management schemes have different security/scaling properties; static shared group keys are not equivalent to Sender Keys or to every group protocol.

          10. Revocation differs from retracting old knowledge

          In the lab, A/B/C have read and saved m0; B then leaves and D joins at membership epoch 2. Fresh sender keys reach current recipients through approved sessions. B should not receive future keys, but its saved m0 does not disappear. Under the selected no-history-sharing policy, D has no old history; explicit authorized history synchronization is a separate operation.

          If senders retain old keys, removing B from relay fan-out does not prevent cryptographic access when B obtains ciphertext elsewhere. Epoch admission rejects honest stale-context sends, but metadata labels do not attest which key actually encrypted a payload. Full protocol/context verification and endpoint assumptions remain necessary. D may need pending-decryption/key repair. Rotation alone does not cure a compromised current endpoint that continues receiving fresh keys.

          11. Attachment lifetime and control-plane isolation

          Upload ciphertext to immutable generations, finalize complete blobs and send protected descriptors bound to generations, integrity and context. Recipients verify before output and handle missing/expired media through explicit UI/retry policies. Keep content keys out of media URLs/access logs. Encrypted payloads still impose size, timing, storage and quota concerns; sensitive thumbnails need suitable protection too.

          Retained/delivered messages do not promise permanent media. Choose message/media retention, deletion/tombstone propagation and orphan cleanup independently. Large downloads bypass latency-sensitive socket relays. Client-side hashes/deduplication do not provide anonymity or prove content safe; integrity depends on authenticated descriptors.

          12. Device identities, history and convergence

          Approved companions are not aliases for one socket. Each has distinct identity/session state, durable inboxes/receipts and relevant outgoing encrypted copies. Directory approvals/consistency prevent unauthorized additions under the threat model. Revocation invalidates future contexts/session/bundle views and defines how queued old envelopes are handled; it does not instantly erase received keys/history.

          History transfers to newly approved devices must be authorized, E2EE and consistent across snapshot/delta boundaries. Chat history, settings and read/deleted state require different merge policies. Stable event IDs/versions and tombstones prevent deleted-state resurrection. Local search runs at endpoints, or through a privacy-preserving design with its own threat model; servers cannot silently decrypt private archives for search.

          13. Backup keys and vault recovery: scope and limits

          Restore the right data to the right device

          1. Select a generation

            Read manifests/versions and recovery points; partial uploads stay unpublished.

          2. Recover secrets

            User-held material or protected vault protocols with attempt/rollback controls.

          3. Verify then restore

            Verify integrity/coherence and approved endpoints; reject mismatched generations.

          4. Synchronize current state

            Apply authorized deltas/tombstones without reviving revoked devices/grants from old backups.

          Key recovery, coherent snapshots and device approval are separate requirements. Successful uploads do not establish restorable backups.

          Encrypted backups combine ciphertext generations, manifests, integrity evidence and selected recovery material. Recovery can use user-held secrets or protected vault/device/password flows. An HSM-based vault can retain secrets under authenticated protected channels and attempt controls. Consequently, universal claims that keys never leave devices or brute force is impossible for every operator are too broad. State HSM/trusted-code, anti-rollback, attempt-limit and recovery-policy assumptions.

          Restores verify generation/coherence/integrity, authenticate approved recipient devices and prevent replay/rollback into revoked authority. Forgotten material, lockout and resets have mode-specific recovery rules; do not infer one universal no-helpdesk guarantee. Backup retention/erasure differs from relay queue garbage collection and user-visible deletion. Test restores with partial data, lost devices and cloud failures rather than only successful uploads.

          14. Partition recovery, privacy and capacity

          Partition owners/session routes need epochs, fencing or equivalent coordination at actual write sinks. Primary/secondary replication does not itself solve split-brain or accepted-data RPO. Backpressure bounds socket mailboxes/queues. Per-user/device/group quotas and jittered reconnects avoid shared authentication/key-directory overload. Define and measure availability of accepted delivery, not merely connected sockets.

          Migrations preserve accepted envelopes/results, metadata versions, protocol state and recovery identities. Shadow comparisons must not duplicate real recipient effects or expose keys. Logs retain minimal safe IDs, versions and reasons rather than plaintext, private keys or raw sensitive membership histories. Track recipient fan-out, key-distribution backlog and missing-key/decryption failures separately.

          16. Worked questions: delivery, encryption and recovery

          Trace one offline direct message, one group epoch transition and one restore before choosing a deep dive. Answers provide design reasoning under the declared scope rather than cryptographic proofs or product guarantees outside its threat model.

          Are server acceptance and recipient reading equivalent?

          No. Server acknowledgment promises selected queue durability; device acknowledgment promises selected local acceptance; reading is a separate explicit signal/policy. Bind receipts to exact devices/envelopes. An offline companion still has a delivery obligation after the phone acknowledges.

          Does one ciphertext make all group work constant?

          Only selected sender payload work becomes smaller after setup. Pairwise distribution and epoch churn still cost resources; server deliveries/egress scale with recipient devices. Logical envelope counts are not equal-cost cryptographic/CPU benchmarks.

          Does removing B from server fan-out suffice?

          Not when future payloads use a key B knows and B can obtain the ciphertext. Establish fresh authenticated sender state and distribution under the protocol. Previously read/saved m0 cannot be revoked; rekeying also cannot cure a compromised current endpoint that remains authorized.

          Do Sender Keys distribute private signing keys?

          No. Recipients receive chain state and public verification material through approved pairwise sessions; private signing keys remain at senders. The complete protocol also defines message-key evolution/erasure, context and replay rules. Do not implement cryptography from a toy lab.

          Does E2EE eliminate metadata and endpoint risk?

          No. Routing, times, sizes, devices and membership metadata need separate controls. Approved recipients can share/report content; compromised endpoints can expose plaintext. Declare directory verification, endpoint trust and backup modes. Having ciphertext does not establish anonymity.

          Does a UUID alone provide exactly-once presentation?

          No. Devices must persist deduplication/state coherently with effects/results and recover before selected acknowledgments. Bind logical identities to senders/conversations and recipient-device envelopes. Changed payload retries need explicit conflicts or revision policies; a UUID alone does not serialize effects.

          Do encrypted backups imply keys never leave devices?

          Not for every mode. A protected HSM vault can retain recovery secrets under a protocol that prevents ordinary frontends from reading them. Declare trust, attempt limits, anti-rollback controls and recovery policies rather than claiming hardware makes all attacks impossible.

          Does a primary-secondary pair ensure exclusive writers?

          No. Partitions and promotions need activation, fencing, quorums or equivalent coordination at actual write sinks. Routing caches are not fences. Recovery must retain accepted envelopes/results under the selected RPO rather than merely starting replacement nodes.
