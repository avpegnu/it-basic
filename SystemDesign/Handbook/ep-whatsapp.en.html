1. Private messengers: relays, archives and endpoint trust

Delivery

  • Acceptance, device durability and reading have distinct guarantees.

Content trust

  • Approved endpoint identities/protocols protect content while metadata has separate controls.

Recovery

  • Queues, history synchronization and backup material have different lifecycles.

This WhatsApp-style messenger covers private direct/group messaging, devices, attachments and backups. Relay-only queues are a policy rather than an E2EE requirement; encrypted server archives are another design. Twelve stages teach architecture rather than describe a current deployment inventory.

Approved endpoints can read/share/report content. Compromised endpoints and saved copies require explicit boundaries. Integrations and reporting may have different plaintext endpoints. Worksheets model counts, histories and assumed possession, not cryptographic implementation.

2. One message has multiple identities and acknowledgements

Accept → persist → deliver → observe

  1. Prepare

    Audited protocol and stable envelopes.

  2. Accept

    Durable obligations under retention/RPO.

  3. Persist

    Recipient persistence/dedup before acknowledgement.

  4. Observe

    Delivery differs from explicit reading.

Transitions require identity and recovery rather than successful socket writes.

Retain logical intent, immutable device-envelope identities, membership context and results. Prepared retries retain envelopes; valid re-encryption retains logical intent under protocol. Use audited state/recovery rather than inventing ratchets that risk reuse or loss.

Server acceptance, device persistence and reading differ. Durable ciphertext may await keys under an explicit contract, without implying reading. Retire exact authenticated device obligations or record explicit retention expiry.

3. Twelve stages: scope, scale and trust boundaries

Follow pressures, choices, costs and exit criteria. Lightweight runtimes do not replace admission or durability, and historical socket records are not current SLAs. Preserve privacy/recovery contracts through platform and device changes.

5. Direct delivery, receipts, groups and restore

Edges describe responsibilities rather than crypto APIs. Verify device-directory identities. Backup vaults can hold protected recovery material under separate protocols, so not every backend component holds only public keys.

Ciphertext delivery, approved devices and recovery

Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

Direct messages reach approved devices

  1. Obtain verified current bundles/approvals rather than arbitrary lists.
  2. Prepare endpoint-encrypted envelopes with stable identities under an audited protocol.
  3. Authenticate senders/devices and validate scoped routing.
  4. Durably retain each device obligation.
  5. Deliver retained ciphertext with idempotent retries.

Persistence, acknowledgements and reading

  1. Acknowledge exact devices/envelopes after durable local acceptance.
  2. Retire completed obligations; missing acknowledgements permit replay.
  3. Report delivery evidence without inferring reading.
  4. Reading follows a separate explicit signal/policy.

Encrypted attachments

  1. Upload complete immutable ciphertext with integrity context.
  2. Send protected descriptors/key context through the message protocol.
  3. Retain small envelopes separately from media bytes.
  4. Fetch bytes, verify protected descriptors and decrypt at endpoints.

Membership epochs and group fan-out

  1. Obtain authenticated membership/device context.
  2. Distribute chain state/verification through approved pairwise sessions.
  3. Send one signed group ciphertext under protocol/epoch context.
  4. Create recipient obligations; egress still scales with recipients.
  5. Companions need valid keys/sessions, not just account labels.

Protected backup and approved restore

  1. Encrypt locally and publish coherent ciphertext generations/manifests.
  2. Use protected recovery protocols without key logging.
  3. Recover through protected approved endpoint channels.
  4. Verify/restore generations without reviving revoked identities.

          7. Durable device receipts and local deduplication

          The fixture distinguishes device obligations, premature acknowledgements and idempotent replay. Persist before acknowledgement and recover recipient state coherently; identity alone does not serialize effects.

          Queues retain ciphertext under explicit durability, expiry and identity. Pending decryption may be durable but is not reading. Expiry needs explicit failure and push notifications are wake hints rather than durable delivery.

          8. Approved identities, metadata and endpoint threat models

          Protect content

          • Use audited protocols with approved identities and context.

          Bound metadata

          • Minimize and govern routing/log data.

          State exclusions

          • Saved plaintext, endpoint compromise and authorized reporting remain explicit boundaries.

          TLS alone does not hide content from its endpoint. E2EE needs approved identities, authenticated setup, evolution and replay/device controls. Directory retrieval is not identity proof; approvals and verification need explicit semantics.

          Routing and timing metadata remain separate privacy concerns. Encryption does not prevent recipient sharing or compromised endpoints. Erased-key secrecy does not erase saved plaintext. Define plaintext endpoints for private messages, integrations and backups.

          9. Sender Keys optimize senders, not recipient delivery

          Distribute chain state and public verification while retaining private signing keys. Payloads evolve/erase keys and remain signed. Membership changes require fresh sender state; distribution may await the next active send. Track sender/group/device generations.

          The lab fixes surviving active senders and per-epoch messages. Recipient counts include companions except origins. Compare pairwise data, sender-key distribution and shared payload counts separately; recipient deliveries remain. Counts are not equivalent cryptographic costs or benchmarks.

          Short/churny workloads may not amortize setup. Actual packets, sizes, setup, signatures, retries and device costs need measurement. Static shared secrets and other group protocols are not interchangeable.

          10. Revocation differs from retracting old knowledge

          The fixture givesA/B/C saved m0, then removesB and addsD. Fresh approved distribution excludesB from future keys without erasing saved history. The selected policy withholds history fromD; authorized history sharing is separate.

          Removing relay recipients alone does not protect ciphertext accessible elsewhere when old keys remain. Epoch guards reject honest stale context but labels do not attest actual encryption. Retain protocol verification and repair missing keys; rotation does not cure a still-authorized compromised endpoint.

          11. Attachment lifetime and control-plane isolation

          Publish complete immutable ciphertext before protected descriptors. Verify before output and handle missing media explicitly. Keep keys out of URLs/logs while governing size/timing and thumbnails.

          Message delivery does not promise eternal media. Define independent retention, deletion and cleanup while separating large transfers. Hashes need authenticated context and do not establish anonymity or general content safety.

          12. Device identities, history and convergence

          Companions have distinct identities, state, inboxes and receipts. Approvals govern additions; revocation updates future contexts and queued-envelope policy without pretending to erase received history.

          Authorize coherent encrypted history synchronization and define per-state merge/deletion semantics. Preserve identities/versions against resurrection. Endpoint search or privacy-preserving alternatives need explicit models rather than silent server decryption.

          13. Backup keys and vault recovery are not magic

          Backups combine versioned data and recovery material. Secrets may be user-held or protected by vault protocols, so universal device-only and brute-force-impossibility claims are too broad. Define hardware/code, rollback and attempt-limit assumptions.

          Restore verified generations into authenticated devices while preventing rollback/revived approval. Recovery/lockout rules depend on mode. Backup retention differs from queue garbage collection and visible deletion; test restores, not only uploads.

          14. Partition recovery, privacy and capacity

          Fence actual ownership rather than only routing. Replication alone does not solve split-brain/RPO. Bound buffers and coordinate quotas/reconnection across authentication and directories. Measure defined delivery availability rather than sockets alone.

          Migrations preserve accepted identity/state without duplicate real effects or key exposure. Log minimally and separate recipient fan-out, distribution backlog and decryption failures.

          16. Worked questions: delivery, encryption and recovery

          Trace offline delivery, membership changes and restore before choosing depth. Answers reason under declared scope rather than prove cryptographic security.

          Are server acceptance and recipient reading equivalent?

          No. Server durability, device acceptance and reading are distinct, device-bound evidence. Phone acknowledgement does not complete an offline companion.

          Does one ciphertext make all group work constant?

          It reduces selected sender work after setup; distribution and recipient deliveries remain. Envelope counts do not establish equivalent compute costs.

          Does removing B from server fan-out suffice?

          Not if B retains future keys and can obtain ciphertext. Refresh authenticated state while recognizing old saved history and current endpoint compromise remain.

          Do Sender Keys distribute private signing keys?

          No. Recipients receive chain state and public verification while private signing remains at the sender, under a complete audited protocol.

          Does E2EE eliminate metadata and endpoint risk?

          No. Govern metadata and endpoint trust separately; approved sharing and compromised devices remain. Verify directories and define backup boundaries.

          Does a UUID alone provide exactly-once presentation?

          No. Persist identities with state/effects and recover before acknowledgements. Bind logical/device context and handle changed payloads explicitly.

          Do encrypted backups imply keys never leave devices?

          Not universally: protected vaults may hold recovery material. Define trust, limits and rollback controls rather than assuming hardware eliminates attacks.

          Does a primary-secondary pair ensure exclusive writers?

          No. Promotion needs actual ownership coordination/fencing and retained accepted state under explicit RPO, not merely new routing.
