Designing a Notification System: correct sends and controlled retries

Follow intents, policies, provider attempts and callbacks; experiment with crash windows, deduplication, preferences, caps and retries without confusing acceptance with delivery.

Four notification boundaries

  1. Accept the intent

    Commit request/result/outbox before returning 202.

  2. Choose and schedule

    Apply preferences, templates, priority, quiet hours and caps.

  3. Send within budgets

    Observe provider quotas, deadlines, retries and unknown outcomes.

  4. Collect evidence

    Verify callbacks, deduplicate and observe delivery/suppression evidence.

One small request can create several deliveries and attempts; preserve identity at each layer.

  • 202, provider acceptance, delivery evidence and reading are distinct milestones.
  • Ingress deduplication does not resolve unknown external-provider timeouts.
  • Separate queues/workers require provider quota reservation and fair scheduling.
  • Check preferences/expiry at the agreed boundary, including retries and DLQ replays.
  • Stable intent/delivery/attempt identities, outboxes and evidence ledgers support contract-correct recovery.

1. Name each notification guarantee precisely

Durably accepted intent

  • 202 means the platform retains the request under its contract, not that a provider or user received it.

Provider accepted

  • The provider accepted processing; store its ID and attempt without inferring display/open.

Delivery evidence

  • Semantics differ by channel; mailbox delivery, device delivery and reading are different events.

A notification platform accepts business intents such as “order shipped,” selects channels and manages policy, scheduling, templates, providers, retries and evidence. Producers should not need to call SMS/email/push providers inside business transactions. Separating the send decision from provider execution makes policy and recovery testable.

The baseline includes push, SMS and email; transactional events and campaigns; preferences, quiet hours, localization and status lookup. In-app inboxes and campaign editors are separate features. Define SLOs at controlled milestones such as acceptance-to-first-attempt or acceptance-to-provider-acceptance, and observe downstream delivery when evidence exists. “Arrives in seconds” is incomplete without acknowledging OS, carrier and mailbox behavior.

2. Size deliveries, attempts and bursts

The example has 50 million pushes, two million SMS and 20 million emails daily: 72 million channel deliveries, not necessarily 72 million API requests. One intent targeting two channels or multiple devices creates several deliveries. A ten-million-recipient campaign over ten minutes needs about 16,667 deliveries/second before retries, far above daily averages.

At 1.5 attempts per delivery, the system creates 108 million attempt records/day. At an illustrative 300 B per record and 30-day retention, attempt payload alone is 972 decimal GB before indexes, replicas, backups and request payloads. Provider quotas, destination limits, outbound connections and channel costs can bottleneck before CPU. Use actual region/provider contracts for pricing rather than one universal SMS rate.

3. Accept intents through an idempotent API

Keys reflect business actions within tenant/producer scope, for example order:123:shipped:v1. Campaign keys often include campaign version, recipient and category; retries preserve the intent key. Clients cannot simply assign critical priority to bypass quotas: producer authorization and policy govern category/priority.

Persist the idempotency result with the durable intent/outbox. SETNX before enqueue is insufficient: a crash after reserving a key but before durable job creation can suppress retries for the TTL. The same key/payload returns the original notification ID; different payloads conflict. Deduplication retention must cover the agreed event replay window rather than merely fit a cache budget.

Accept a logical intent; the platform decides a delivery plan under policy.

        4. Durable intents, outboxes and channel workers

        A producer can use a transactional outbox to couple business commits with intent publication. Notification ingress separately needs atomic intent + idempotency result + its own planning/outbox. These are different boundaries, not one transaction across all microservices. Relays can duplicate events, so consumers retain stable identities.

        Separate logical intents, channel deliveries, provider attempts and provider callbacks. A worker claim can expire while a process pauses; the old worker may still call a provider. CAS/fencing protects ledger updates but does not automatically prevent an external side effect unless the provider checks a token/key. Claim timeouts, provider deadlines and recovery must account for this instead of treating a local lock as proof against duplicate sends.

        From business intent to a provider and delivery evidence

        Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

        Accept an intent and return 202

        1. Send an intent and an idempotency key scoped to tenant/producer and business event.
        2. Atomically insert the intent/result and outbox; a same-payload duplicate returns the existing notification ID.
        3. Commit reaches the durability contract. No provider has been called yet.
        4. Return 202 Accepted with a notification ID for lookup.
        5. The relay reads the committed outbox and publishes a planning job; retries can duplicate it.

        Policy permits and the provider accepts

        1. A worker receives/claims a delivery job with a stable delivery ID.
        2. Check current preferences, category, quiet hours, caps and expiry.
        3. Allow sending; record the policy version/reason rather than exempting every transactional intent.
        4. Record the attempt/claim and render version before the external call; this is not provider success.
        5. Send after quota reservation; preserve idempotency identity when the provider supports suitable semantics.
        6. The provider returns acceptance and a provider message ID; this does not prove mailbox/device receipt or reading.
        7. Record provider acceptance against the correct attempt, then complete the job.

        Preferences change while a job is waiting

        1. An older job is dequeued after a delay.
        2. Recheck consent/preferences before external send under the defined policy.
        3. The category/channel is no longer allowed or its deadline has expired.
        4. Record suppression/expiry with a reason and policy version; do not call the provider.

        Provider timeout: the outcome is unknown

        1. The worker sends a request; the provider may accept it before the connection is lost.
        2. A timeout does not prove nonprocessing; this trace represents the worker observing a timeout.
        3. Record an unknown attempt, not permanent failure or delivery.
        4. Query/reconcile or retry under the idempotency/deadline contract. Switching providers can duplicate the send.

        Callbacks and recipient evidence

        1. The provider/OS/mail server performs the next step outside the platform’s full control.
        2. Delivery/bounce/complaint or other channel events may be delayed, duplicated or out of order.
        3. Verify, deduplicate and store attempt facts; derive status without discarding prior evidence.
        Product / campaign
        Produces intents with stable business identities. Campaigns expand audiences in pages under budgets, not millions of recipients in memory.
        Notification API
        Authenticates producers, validates schema/category and idempotency scope, and durably accepts before 202.
        Intent + delivery ledger
        Authoritative intents, delivery plans, attempts and outbox. Callback facts are deduplicated and linked to the correct provider attempt.
        Preferences + policy
        Consent/category, channels, quiet hours, timezone, suppression and caps. Rechecks changeable policies before sending.
        Channel × priority
        The outbox relay publishes delivery jobs. Scheduling and quotas isolate urgent capacity from bulk work.
        Delivery worker
        Claims jobs, rechecks policy/expiry, renders a template version, reserves provider budget and sends with stable identity.
        External provider
        Has its own quota and idempotency contract. A timeout may leave the result unknown; acceptance does not imply reading.
        Retry / DLQ
        Schedules retries within deadlines/budgets. DLQs need reasons, ownership and replay policies, not a resend-everything button.
        Callback ingestion
        Verifies signatures/authentication, deduplicates events, maps provider IDs to attempts and stores out-of-order facts.
        Recipient / mailbox
        Delivery, display and opening are distinct milestones; OSs, carriers and mailboxes can block, delay or omit telemetry.

        5. Ingress deduplication does not remove provider ambiguity

        At-least-once processing with idempotent local state is not end-to-end exactly-once delivery. If a provider accepts and its response is lost, the platform cannot know whether to resend. Adequate provider idempotency using the same key/payload within its retention window can deduplicate acceptance. Otherwise choose query/reconciliation, duplicate risk or missed-send risk by category. Neither declare every external exactly-once effect impossible nor claim a queue alone provides it.

        Push collapsing/coalescing is not generic idempotency: depending on the platform it may replace pending messages without recalling displayed notifications or deduplicating all historical retries. Rely on stable identities only under documented provider semantics; a different provider usually does not share deduplication state.

        6. Inspect every gate instead of calling everything sent

        Ingress dedup removes logical replays, preferences decide permission to send, caps control budgets/admission, workers create attempts and providers report acceptance. Record reasons at each gate so support can explain nonreceipt. Suppression is not provider failure, a DLQ is not delivery, and retries are not new notifications.

        The model replays the same stream under each configuration. Its cap counts admitted marketing intents per channel and retries do not spend another slot. Production may cap by recipient/category/window; define reservation, commitment or refund boundaries and prevent read-then-write races among workers.

        7. Separate queues are only half of isolation

        A FIFO queue containing ten million campaign emails before a password reset creates head-of-line blocking. Channel × priority queues/workers reduce contention, but OTPs still stall if bulk traffic exhausts the same provider quota. Reserve throughput/admission budgets at the appropriate provider/account/pool level, throttle campaigns and bound expansion rates. Separate accounts/domains/IPs do not create quota if the provider enforces a shared higher-level limit.

        Strict priority can starve bulk work under sustained urgent load. Choose weighted scheduling, reserved minima, aging or deadline-aware admission based on SLOs. Budget recovery retries and keep DLQ replay from consuming all fresh-traffic capacity. Isolation also covers shared template services, preference stores and connection pools, not just queue topics.

        8. Preferences, quiet hours, expiry and fatigue

        Policies attach to category and purpose, not just channel switches. Password resets, security alerts and marketing differ, but labelling every event transactional must not bypass preferences. Consent/preferences are versioned data with audit and cache update paths. Recheck before sending so delayed jobs do not use stale opt-ins, and define the race cutoff when a preference changes during an external call.

        Quiet hours use the user’s IANA timezone and local calendar; DST days are not always 24 hours. Define repeated/missing local-time behavior and timezone changes. Schedule with UTC due_at while retaining timezone/policy versions for explanations; reevaluate at execution when required. An OTP expiring during quiet hours needs explicit suppression or category/channel policy, not delivery of an unusable code the next morning.

        Digests aggregate social events by recipient/category/window with deterministic identity and cutoffs. Do not merge events requiring independent content/order just because they share a user. Frequency caps must define whether they count admission, provider acceptance or delivery. Admission caps can charge DLQ work, while success-only caps still need reservations to bound concurrent attempts.

        9. Retry by error class, deadline and budget

        Decide after a failed attempt

        1. Classify the outcome

          Accepted, retryable, invalid/revoked or unknown; retain evidence.

        2. Check policy and deadline

          Do preferences/expiry still allow it? Is retry/cost budget available?

        3. Schedule or finish

          Schedule with jitter or terminate/dead-letter with reasons and replay guards.

        Unknown outcomes require reconciliation/idempotency policy; a timeout is not proof of nonsending.

        Classify failures using provider/channel contracts. Transient server errors/throttling can be retryable; invalid payloads or invalidated tokens usually need repair/quarantine rather than identical retries. Authentication/configuration failures are not necessarily permanent for every job: pause the provider, repair credentials and replay within deadlines. Respect Retry-After when present, use exponential backoff with jitter and attempt/deadline budgets, and use delayed queues/schedulers rather than sleeping a worker/thread for minutes.

        Check deadlines before each send and provider failover. An expired or revoked intent must not be replayed merely to drain an outage backlog. DLQs need reasons, attempt history, payload/version, expiry and an operational owner. Replays pass through the same policy/idempotency path rather than copying records into new intents with new keys. DLQs are not the only outcome: exhausted/expired jobs may be auditable terminal states that must not be resent.

        10. Template versions, localization and token hygiene

        Pin template versions, locales and input schemas so retries do not unexpectedly change content. Validate rendering and escape for email/HTML/text with URL policies; avoid unnecessary secrets/PII on push lock screens. Locale fallback must be deterministic and tested. Detect render failures early or record explained terminal/retry-after-repair states instead of calling a provider with an empty body and marking it sent.

        Push tokens rotate, apps are uninstalled and user ownership changes after logout/login. Authenticate registrations by device/user/app/environment. An invalid-token callback disables only the appropriate registration/version rather than removing a new token because an old callback arrives late. Restrict token logging/access and retention; a token is not a permanent user identity.

        11. Ledgers and callbacks preserve evidence, not one linear status

        An intent holds producer scope, business key, payload hash, category, template version, schedule/expiry and policy context. A delivery is a recipient/channel/target plan with a stable delivery ID. Attempts hold provider, attempt ID, idempotency key, start time, timeout/deadline, response/provider ID and outcome. Do not grow an unbounded attempt array in a hot row; use append-only attempt/event records with support indexes.

        Callbacks can be out of order or duplicated. Store provider event IDs, event time, receipt time, authentication results and attempt mapping; derive a view from facts rather than overwriting status with the latest callback. Complaints may follow delivery, opens can come from proxies/privacy features, and not every channel exposes delivery/open evidence. Unknown is a valid state and must not become false success to improve a dashboard.

        12. Provider failover, budgets and safe operations

        Guardrails before a burst

        • Paged audience expansion, reserved quotas, campaign budgets and worker-level pausing.

        Controlled recovery

        • Rate-limited replay, policy/expiry rechecks and data-error quarantine.

        Know what remains unknown

        • Expose unknown provider outcomes and missing telemetry honestly; do not promise exactly-once end-user delivery.

        Fail over based on health, region, capability, deliverability and quotas, not just HTTP availability. Use provider-level circuit breakers/bulkheads and retry budgets, and reserve fallback capacity before an incident. If A times out with an unknown outcome and B is called, the recipient can receive both. Preserve one logical delivery ID in the ledger without assuming cross-provider deduplication.

        Per-tenant/campaign spend budgets, rate limits, review policies and kill switches limit runaway SMS/email loops. Kill switches stop queued jobs at execution rather than merely refusing new requests. Consent/unsubscribe/retention requirements become versioned policies defined by the responsible product/legal teams for their applicable scope, not one universal rule inferred from the transactional label.

        13. Milestone funnels and crash-window tests

        Dashboards separate input requests, unique accepted intents, planned deliveries, preference suppression, caps, expiry, attempts, provider acceptance, delivery evidence, complaints and open/click evidence. Denominators differ: attempts can exceed deliveries, and duplicate events are not new users. Use provider/region/category/template/tenant dimensions with bounded cardinality; trace IDs must not expose sensitive payloads.

        Alert on urgent queue age/deadlines, acceptance latency, retry amplification, unknown-outcome age, DLQ reasons/rates, invalid-token trends and budget burn. Test crashes after key reservation before durable jobs, after provider acceptance before ledger updates, stale workers after lease expiry, opt-outs while queued, out-of-order callbacks and replays after expiry. Verify invariants/no silent loss at durable boundaries and reasons for intentional suppression rather than merely counting sent events.

        14. Scenario questions with conditional answers

        A strong answer names boundaries and failure modes before adding retries or queues. Use these scenarios to explain deadline, duplication, preference and budget trade-offs.

        SETNX dedup succeeds, then the process dies before enqueue. Is that safe?

        No. Retries can be suppressed even though no durable job exists. Atomic intent/result plus an outbox, or a recoverable state machine, links reservation to actual work. A separate key TTL only bounds the failure interval; it does not prove the intent cannot be lost.

        Can a local ledger guarantee no duplicates after a provider timeout following acceptance?

        Not by local checks alone. The provider outcome is unknown and a retry can create another acceptance. Contract-appropriate provider idempotency or status reconciliation can help; otherwise choose a category-specific trade-off. A collapse key is not automatically an exactly-once key, and switching providers can remove deduplication protection.

        OTP is slow during campaigns despite priority queues. What should you inspect?

        Inspect shared provider/account quotas, connection pools, preference/template stores and campaign expansion. Separate queues do not create new provider throughput. Reserve urgent capacity at the actual quota boundary, throttle bulk, schedule fairly and measure urgent queue age/acceptance latency separately.

        A user opts out after a job is queued. Should it still send?

        Recheck at execution under category policy and record suppression reason/version. If the external call has begun, define the race cutoff; deleting a queue entry cannot recall an email/SMS already sent. Kill switches/cancellations must guard execution as well as ingress.

        Should every DLQ item be replayed after an outage?

        No. Filter expired/revoked work, invalid payloads/tokens and already-resolved intents; preserve stable identities and recheck policy. Replay needs its own quota so it cannot overload fresh traffic. Configuration errors may be repaired and replayed, whereas expired terminal work needs audit rather than resending.

        Does a delivered callback prove a user read an email?

        No. Delivery is a downstream milestone rather than reading, and opens can be affected by proxies/privacy features. Name evidence precisely, allow unknown states and avoid one SENT status for all channels. Verify and deduplicate callbacks, including late ones.

        Why might a template retry send different content than its first attempt?

        Rendering the latest template from mutable data can change a retry’s body. Pin the template version, locale and input snapshot, or explicitly define fields that are reevaluated. Changing payloads under the same provider idempotency key can conflict, so content identity and retry contracts must align.

        Why do success-only caps still need reservations?

        Concurrent workers can all observe a count below the cap and send before callbacks arrive, exceeding it. Atomically reserve allowance before calls, commit/release by outcome and handle unknowns separately, or choose simpler documented admission-cap semantics. A delivery retry must not arbitrarily spend the cap again.
