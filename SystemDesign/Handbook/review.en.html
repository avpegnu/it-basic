Daily Review: retrieve knowledge and space your practice

Review canonical questions and visual summaries with filters, due-first sessions, self-grading, undo, transparent intervals, forecasts and local backups without equating confidence with mastery.

A useful review loop

  1. Recall

    Answer or sketch before revealing.

  2. Check

    Check invariants, assumptions and caveats.

  3. Grade

    Grade recalled performance honestly.

  4. Space / apply

    Return later and apply ideas to new cases.

Aim to explain and apply rather than recognize familiar answers.

  • Retrieve before revealing, check omissions and explain ideas through examples and failures.
  • The transparent schedule does not predict exact forgetting times or interview success.
  • Again becomes due after ten elapsed minutes rather than being immediately replayed as though time passed.
  • Due counts follow filters, daily new limits span the deck, and content revisions can reopen cards.
  • Separate scheduling preserves old ratings/bookmarks; export before important data changes.

1. Practice retrieval rather than recognition alone

Before revealing, explain an answer, sketch its flow and identify a failure. Then compare and correct omissions. Recognition after reading differs from unaided retrieval. Retrieval and spacing can support delayed retention, with effects depending on tasks, feedback and practice rather than a universal improvement factor or optimal schedule.

Cards complement coding, debugging, measurement and design reasoning. Easy recall does not establish secure code, correct transactions or interview readiness. Repeated misses can signal that a card needs narrower scope or deeper study rather than more new cards.

2. Your review deck and schedule

Filter sources/topics and set daily/session limits. Sessions prioritize the oldest due cards, then eligible unseen cards. Reveal before self-grading; undo restores the latest grade in the session, while skipping does not grade. Previewing future/unseen cards does not alter schedules.

With focus on card content, Space reveals, 1–4 grade and U undoes. Buttons retain native keys, and input/select typing is unaffected. Reload returns to the dashboard while retaining saved grades. Missed cards are not silently requeued before they become due.

3. Reuse canonical content with stable identities

Topic cards reuse stable question identities, answers, explanations, follow-ups and links. Handbook cards retain lesson/section context; visual cards use existing overview flows and takeaways. No model generates fresh answers during review.

Revision checks prevent changed content from inheriting old recall evidence automatically. Revised cards become unseen for queue selection, with old records retained until grading. Identities and state do not depend on the viewing language, though content changes can affect revisions. Scheduling does not validate source correctness.

4. Grade what you recalled before revealing

Again means recall failed or missed the main idea; Hard reflects difficult or uncertain recall; Good means explaining the main idea and conditions; Easy reflects clear recall with caveats. These are practice labels, not validated assessments. Revisit unclear explanations rather than grading highly because you just read them.

5. Inspect the explicit interval ladder

The teaching ladder is ten minutes, then 1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60, 120 and 180 days. New Good reviews move to one day, Easy to three, and Hard/Again to ten minutes. The 180-day cap is a product heuristic, not an implementation of a calibrated memory model or a guarantee of retention.

Due dates use the actual grading time. At a seven-day level, Hard schedules 3.5 days, Good 14 days, Easy 30 days, and Again ten minutes at level zero. Days mean 24 elapsed hours without midnight rounding. Previewing does not execute scheduling transitions.

6. Prioritize due cards and bound daily introductions

The daily introduction allowance counts newly graded cards/revisions across the entire deck, regardless of filters. Due counts and forecasts use matching revisions within the filtered pool. Sessions prioritize oldest due cards, then interleave eligible new cards across sources within the session cap.

Skipping an unseen card does not consume the introduction allowance. Exhausting it still permits due reviews and schedule-free previews. The legacy-review filter reads existing topic ratings without changing those ratings or bookmarks.

7. Separate clocks, local days and forecasts

Due dates are epoch timestamps with elapsed intervals. Daily counters use the local calendar at review time; clock/timezone changes can affect displayed dates and limits. This personal browser tool is not an authoritative attendance or financial system and does not synchronize clocks across devices.

Forecasts use seven rolling 24-hour bins rather than seven calendar dates. The first includes overdue cards, and all bins count existing matching scheduled records in the filtered deck. They do not predict future grades, new introductions or recall success. Browser time refreshes periodically.

8. Undo restores exact prior state within bounded history

Grades retain prior card records and day counters. Undo restores exact snapshots rather than merely changing due dates or rebuilding counters from truncated logs. A bounded history of 50 grades leaves aggregate counters intact when older history is evicted.

The UI undoes grades from the current session; deliberate imports clear undo lineage. Skips and previews are not graded evidence. Review counts and lapse rates are selected self-reports rather than independent measures of knowledge.

9. Follow evidence boundaries through practice

For timeout/retry questions, recall unknown outcomes, stable intents, sink cooperation and visible-output boundaries. Compare against explanations and trace a lost response rather than only reciting idempotency. Apply the idea to another scenario to test transfer.

10. Preserve separate local state with backup and import preflight

Scheduling uses separate storage without overwriting course progress, bookmarks or topic ratings. Browser restrictions or clearing can remove data, so export backups. Reload retains saved grades but not an active session position. Both languages share record identities and schedules.

Imports validate format, identities, records, counters, bounded history and a five-megabyte limit before showing a preview. Explicit application replaces scheduling without blindly adding overlapping counters. Invalid data preserves state. Corruption and cross-tab changes pause saving until deliberate recovery.

11. Adjust practice volume without optimizing card counts

Start with a manageable number of introductions. If backlog grows, pause new cards and revisit explanations. Broad cards can be refined in source content rather than mutating answers inside scheduling. Linked lessons retain the context needed for deeper study.

Combine recall with coding, SQL, tracing and mock discussions. Do not treat a heuristic as an optimal prescription. Investigate and correct source answers when needed; confidence cannot repair incorrect content.

12. Check your understanding of review mechanics

Does Again immediately return at the end of the queue?

Not in scheduled mode: it is due ten minutes later. Extra immediate practice must not pretend that time elapsed.

Does changing filters reset the daily new-card allowance?

No. Introduction limits span the deck, while due counts and forecasts follow filters.

Can two device backups be merged by adding counters?

Overlapping histories can double-count. Imports use explicit replacement rather than claiming exact cross-device merges.

Does Easy establish mastery?

No. A self-report on a selected card does not establish mastery; explanations, applications and failures need separate evidence.
