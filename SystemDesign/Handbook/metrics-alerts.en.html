Designing Metrics & Alerting: cardinality, rollups and evidence

Explore cardinality, gauge downsampling and pending/firing/no-data states; design ingestion, retention, histogram queries and routing under explicit semantics.

  3. Query and evaluate

  4. Route and recover

1. Metrics are operational evidence rather than chart lines

Identities and units

  • Types, labels/resources/scopes and temporality determine valid merges.

Cost and fidelity

  • Cardinality/churn, retention, query work and fidelity all need budgets.

Evidence and action

  • True/false/no-data/query-error states differ, as do firing, notification and human action.

The system collects service and host metrics, supports time-range queries, precomputes measurements through recording rules and routes alerts to on-call responders. Before selecting a TSDB, define active series, sampling intervals, retention and query workloads. This lesson assumes ten million active series, ten-second sampling, bounded recent-data queries below one second and coarser retention up to two years. These are design targets for calculation rather than product benchmarks.

Metrics commonly answer how many errors occur or where latency is increasing. Logs retain individual event details; traces follow requests across components. These signals complement one another but have different storage and cost models. Exemplars can link histogram observations to related traces without placing request IDs on every metric series.

Distinguish accepted data, durable storage and query-visible data. Define tolerated loss, ingest-to-query delay, partial-result behavior and alert-delivery targets. A separate monitoring cluster reduces application dependencies but can still share networks, DNS, cloud accounts or pagers. Test end-to-end behavior from another failure domain rather than checking only whether the monitoring process is alive.

2. Samples/second exclude metadata and query work

Ten million active series sampled every ten seconds produce one million logical samples per second, or 86.4 billion per day. Budgeting sixteen B for a raw timestamp/value pair gives 1.3824 decimal TB/day. If workload measurements justify a two-B compressed planning estimate, payload becomes 172.8 GB/day. Fifteen days with two copies require 5.184 TB for that payload. Two B must be verified against actual data rather than treated as guaranteed compression.

These figures exclude indexes, WALs, in-memory heads, compaction, metadata and query CPU. Two HA collectors scraping the same target may multiply physical ingestion separately from storage replication. A classic histogram with B bucket series plus sum and count creates B + 2 series per label set; native histograms have different representations and costs. Many active series consume head resources, while churn creates new identities even without increasing concurrent series. Bytes/sample or retention alone cannot predict total cost.

3. Counters, gauges and histograms have different merges

Prometheus identifies a series by metric name and its full label set. OpenTelemetry additionally describes resources, instrumentation scopes, units, data types, attributes and temporality. Exporters must preserve meaning when mapping these models rather than merely rename fields. A request counter accumulates events and may reset on restart; a gauge measures a current level such as connection count; histograms describe observation distributions through counts, sums and compatible buckets or schemas.

Delta points describe changes over an interval; cumulative points accumulate from a start time. Conversion requires state and policies for resets, gaps and overlapping intervals. Independent cumulative writers have separate start times and resets: deleting their instance labels does not create one valid counter. Retain writer identities until the correct aggregation boundary or use SDK views/collectors with suitable reaggregation. Counter rates therefore cannot be obtained by averaging values as if they were gauges.

        5. Buffers, shards and query layers have their own trade-offs

        Collectors discover targets, pull exporters or accept pushes, then validate and forward data. Kafka may provide buffering or fan-out, but local WAL and remote-write queues can also serve designs without Kafka. An ACK after durable queue acceptance proves only that queue boundary, not TSDB query visibility. Large backlogs can make dashboards and alerts stale even while processes remain healthy.

        Sharding by tenant and series requires measured distribution to avoid hot shards. Broad queries fan out across shards, so ingestion throughput does not guarantee query latency. HA collectors and replicas need explicit deduplication or election rules rather than blindly removing replica labels and merging independent writers. Query cache keys must include tenant, expression, step, time range, resolution and relevant generations. Dashboard caches also need freshness bounds before their results are used for paging.

        6. Cardinality counts observed identities; products are bounds

        Cardinality counts actually observed series identities. Multiplying distinct label-value counts gives an upper bound on possible combinations rather than an actual count when labels correlate or combinations never appear. Raw URLs, user IDs, request IDs and rapidly changing deployment IDs can increase active series or churn. Hashing each ID to a distinct value largely preserves cardinality and does not inherently guarantee anonymity.

        Using /users/{id} instead of raw paths such as /users/123 often preserves useful information without one series per user. Budget active series, new series and sample rates per tenant, attributing costs to metrics and owners. Rejection, dropping or aggregation after a limit must be observable. Dropping labels may collide independent writers, so valid aggregation must understand types and temporality. The experiment separates possible combinations from assumed observed coverage.

        7. Failed scrapes and silence have different meanings

        Pull collection reveals which targets are expected and whether scrapes succeed. Failures can arise from networks, authentication, exporters or timeouts without proving that the service process died. A target disappearing from discovery also differs from a known target returning up = 0. Push suits some networks and short-lived workloads, but needs heartbeat and lifecycle policies so old samples do not imply healthy jobs.

        Retry queues need bounded memory/disk, backpressure, drop metrics and durable-acceptance contracts. Application instrumentation also needs bounded overhead during backend failures. Engines must define old timestamps, out-of-order samples, duplicates and staleness. A missed scrape does not necessarily remove a query result immediately because lookback or range selectors may still find older samples. Design no-data alerts against actual query semantics rather than only the last scrape request.

        8. Downsample by metric type rather than averages alone

        A TSDB may use an in-memory head, durable WAL, compressed immutable blocks and indexes. Writing to a userspace buffer does not satisfy every crash-durability contract; identify the ACK boundary. Timestamp delta/delta-of-delta and float XOR exploit regularity, but results depend on workload. Compaction and cold-tier transfers need consistent publication/manifests; delete hot blocks only after verified cold publication and usable query routing.

        For regularly sampled gauges, bucket minima, maxima, sums and counts preserve extrema and sample-weighted means. Averaging bucket means weights buckets equally and fails when the last bucket has fewer samples; divide total sums by total counts instead. These fields still lose spike timing/duration, arbitrary percentiles and reset-aware counter rates. Time-weighted means additionally require durations and gap policies. Each retention tier must state which queries remain meaningful after downsampling.

        9. Merge distributions before quantiles instead of averaging p99

        Instance p99s cannot be averaged into a global p99. Suppose A receives 1,000 requests taking one ms each, while B receives ten taking 100 ms each. Nearest-rank p99 over all 1,010 raw requests is still one ms, while the average of instance p99s is 50.5 ms. A quantile value lacks the distribution and weighting information needed for merging; even weighted p99 averages do not solve the general problem. Summary sums/counts can aggregate according to their own semantics, but do not make quantile fields mergeable.

        Classic histograms require compatible bucket boundaries and preservation of the le label while summing bucket-count rates. Native histograms also have schema/resolution compatibility rules. Estimate quantiles after merging distributions. Buckets, sketches and sampling introduce error, so the result is not universally an exact raw percentile. The PromQL example uses seconds as indicated by the metric name; multiply by 1,000 at presentation time if milliseconds are needed.

        histogram_quantile(
  0.99,
  sum by (service, le) (
    rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m])
  )
)

        Classic-histogram example; validate buckets, labels, units and traffic populations before alerting.

        10. Query completeness, caching and noisy neighbors

        A range query needs start, end and step, but reading behavior also depends on range selectors, lookback and resolution. Combining hot/cold tiers must avoid overlap double-counting and calculations unsupported by coarse rollups. A timed-out shard must produce warnings or failure according to contract, rather than silently presenting partial results as complete. Dashboards may allow best-effort behavior, while paging and SLO validation often require stricter completeness and freshness.

        Recording rules reduce query work by storing precomputed aggregates, at the cost of state, evaluation lag, ownership and version dependencies. Tenant quotas on time ranges, matched series, concurrency and work, alongside cancellation and fair scheduling, prevent broad dashboard regexes from starving alert evaluators. Measure ingestion, heads/indexes, churn, storage, compaction and query/rule CPU separately; optimizing payload bytes alone does not optimize the system.

        11. Pending durations use elapsed time for stable alert identities

        When an expression returns an element satisfying its condition, that label set identifies an alert instance and starts pending. With for: 3m, the same identity must remain active through the required evaluations and at least three minutes must elapse from first true. If evaluations run every minute and first true is at minute seven, firing begins no earlier than ten. Evaluations at seven, eight and nine span only two minutes. Put changing current values in annotations rather than labels that could create new identities.

        False conditions, absent expression results and query errors need distinct policies. The demo removes the result at minute ten, exposes NO_DATA and resets pending; it does not equate a missed scrape with immediate PromQL sample absence. keep_firing_for retains an already-firing alert after its condition disappears, while for governs initial firing. Both differ from routing group_wait, group_interval or repeat_interval. A firing alert does not prove responder delivery or acknowledgment.

        12. Burn rates need denominators and error-budget contracts

        Burn rate measures how quickly the error budget is consumed relative to its allowed rate: observed bad fraction divided by allowed bad fraction. A 99.9% SLO permits 0.001 bad fraction; one-percent errors imply burn rate ten. Sustaining that rate for an hour within a thirty-day, 720-hour window consumes 10/720, about 1.39% of the budget. Multi-window alerts combine short and long evidence to balance fast detection and false positives, with thresholds and page/ticket choices tied to product impact.

        Define eligible requests and bad events. No traffic, very few samples, absent numerator series and telemetry outages do not all mean zero errors. The YAML illustrates one error-fraction threshold rather than a complete multi-window SLO policy. Numerator zero fallback applies only where total series exist; the positive-denominator condition excludes no-traffic from this alert, requiring separate policies for no traffic and missing telemetry. Validate rules and replay data before rollout.

        groups:
  - name: example-http
    rules:
      - record: service:http_requests:rate5m
        expr: sum by (service) (rate(http_requests_total[5m]))
      - record: service:http_errors:rate5m
        expr: |
          sum by (service) (rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[5m]))
          or on (service) (0 * service:http_requests:rate5m)
      - alert: ElevatedHttpErrorFraction
        expr: |
          (service:http_errors:rate5m / service:http_requests:rate5m > 0.01)
          and on (service) (service:http_requests:rate5m > 0)
        for: 3m
        labels:
          severity: page
        annotations:
          summary: "Elevated HTTP errors for {{ $labels.service }}"
          description: "Observed fraction: {{ $value }}"

        Adapt labels/names/eligibility to instrumentation; manage missing-data rules and ownership/routing separately.

        13. Firing does not establish that a human received a page

        From evidence to action

        1. Evaluate

          Use fresh compatible evidence and stable identities.

        2. Pending and firing

          Apply duration policies for detection needs.

        3. Group and route

          Deduplicate, inhibit and apply owned silences/escalation.

        4. Act and review

          Acknowledge, act, recover and review quality.

        Rule timers, routing timers and human acknowledgment are distinct layers.

        Routing groups related alerts, deduplicates HA evaluators using stable normalized labels, inhibits symptom floods from one incident and applies owned, expiring silences. Handle evaluator replica labels according to alert identity policy so two evaluators do not generate independent pages. Transports have retries and receipts; responder acknowledgment and escalation are subsequent workflows rather than implications of a successful webhook response.

        Prioritize pages for user-impacting symptoms or actionable SLO budget burn. Cause signals may belong on dashboards or tickets, or justify pages when they identify a concrete preventive action; not all cause alerts are forbidden. Each page needs ownership, severity, runbooks and context. Assess actionability, detection time and missed incidents rather than merely reduced page counts. External dead-man checks or canaries detect silent monitoring failures, but shared dependencies and alternative escalation still need testing.

        14. Governance and self-monitoring are part of the system

        Protect ingestion, queries, rules and configuration by tenant. Govern PII, units and metric-name stability; keep secrets, card data and user IDs out of unbounded labels or public dashboards. Instrumentation changes need linting and cardinality budgets. Rule changes need versions, replay/unit tests and canaries. Alongside application metrics, monitor collector drops, ingest-to-query lag, rule evaluation lag, query failures and actual responder notification delivery.

        15. Interview questions on metric meaning and alert quality

        Explain series identity, units, temporality, query populations and freshness before selecting a TSDB. For alerts, draw first true, evaluation cadence, firing time and missing-data/query-error policies on a timeline. Use the questions below to test both calculation semantics and the operational path after firing.

        Does adding user IDs always multiply series by exactly one million?

        Products bound possible combinations; observed sparse/correlated combinations determine actual counts. IDs/churn can still exhaust budgets, and hashing preserves distinctness. Apply tenant limits and semantically correct views.

        Does dropping instance labels correctly merge two counters?

        Not automatically. Independent cumulative writers have separate resets/timestamps; collisions are not aggregation. Use appropriate views/sums or retain identities to the correct query boundary.

        Can extrema/sums/counts recover all rates and p99s?

        No. Gauge sample means/extrema survive, while counter resets/boundaries, distributions and time weights need additional information. Merge compatible distributions for estimated quantiles rather than average instance p99s.

        With three-minute duration and first true at seven, does minute evaluation fire at nine or ten?

        No earlier than ten with continuously active identity/conditions: observations at seven/eight/nine span two minutes. Missing data or changing identities can reset/delay durations; routing/human timers differ.

        Does a failed scrape mean the service is down?

        It indicates collection failure, potentially from networks/auth/exporters/timeouts. Correlate discovery/black-box signals without treating silence as healthy zeros.

        Do two B/sample suffice to predict costs?

        It is a measured-planning assumption. Heads/indexes/WALs/churn/histograms/replicas/compaction/query CPU/retention also cost resources; payload bytes are not the whole bill.

        Does buffering in Kafka guarantee fresh alerts?

        No. Durable backlogs preserve data while delaying query evidence. Track acceptance-to-visibility, rule freshness and bounded backlog/drop/recovery policies.

        Do fewer pages prove better alerting?

        Not when incidents are missed. Measure actionability, incident precision/recall, detection, ownership/runbooks and delivery, matching SLO/volume/no-data policies to impact.
