1. Begin with units and an assumption sheet

Decimal and binary bytes

  • 1 GB = 10⁹ bytes; 1 GiB = 2³⁰ bytes.
  • Convert to the same units before dividing by machine capacity.

Rates and totals

  • Requests/s differs from requests/day; bandwidth differs from stored bytes.
  • Multiply a rate by duration for a total; divide a total by duration for a rate.

Average and peak

  • Averages describe total work; peaks describe busy periods.
  • A peak factor is an assumption to validate, not a universal constant.

Record DAU, operations per user per day, read/write ratio, payload and retention before calculating. DAU is not concurrent users; being online does not imply actively sending requests. One app opening may trigger five API calls, while one API call may access three databases or create thousands of background writes.

Use consistent units: a day has 86,400 seconds, decimal GB is 10^9 bytes and GiB is 2^30 bytes. Use 1,000-byte KB for decimal calculations or explicitly use 1,024-byte KiB. The GB/GiB difference may be smaller than workload uncertainty, but mixing units makes results harder to audit.

Use plausible ranges rather than false precision: 0.5–2 KB average payloads, peak factors of three to six and 30-day retention. Calculate a baseline and vary one input at a time to identify sensitive decisions. You do not need every hardware latency memorized to detect excessive fan-out.

2. Convert DAU to average and peak RPS

Assume five million DAU, each with twenty reads and two writes per day. Reads total 100 million/day, about 1,157/s; writes total ten million/day, about 116/s. Read and write peak factors of four and three give roughly 4,630 read/s and 347 write/s. Use separate peak factors when their bursts differ.

Peak factors are not natural constants. Time zones, campaigns, scheduled notifications and hot events can create bursts shorter than a one-minute metric window. Compare per-second peaks with instantaneous capacity and longer bursts with queue/buffer capacity; ordinary averages can hide 30 seconds of sustained overload.

daily_reads = DAU * reads_per_user_per_day
average_read_RPS = daily_reads / 86400
peak_read_RPS = average_read_RPS * read_peak_factor
background_writes = source_writes * eligible_recipients

Keep reads, writes and background work separate to expose amplification.

3. Storage: payloads, indexes, replicas and retention

Ten million writes/day × 1 KB × 30 days gives 300 GB of payload. Assuming indexes/metadata add 50%, logical storage is 450 GB; three physical copies require about 1.35 TB before compression, allocation, compaction and headroom. Replicas increase storage but do not linearly multiply write capacity because writes must still replicate.

Which data has that retention? Message bodies, attachments, audit events, tombstones and indexes have different lifetimes. Soft deletion does not immediately free space; backups and replicas may retain data longer. Frequent updates add write-ahead logs, versions or LSM compaction work beyond final-row size.

Do not divide 1.35 TB by SSD capacity and declare the node count. You need query latency, read/write IOPS, RAM working sets, skew, replication/failure policy and maintenance headroom. A dataset fitting one disk may still need several nodes for throughput or availability.

4. Bandwidth and media may dominate

At 4,630 peak reads/s with a 20 KB average response, payload egress is about 92.6 MB/s or 741 Mb/s before protocol/TLS overhead. Bits and bytes differ by a factor of eight. JSON responses are tiny compared with video: one million views of 10 MB videos create about 10 TB of egress despite little metadata growth.

A CDN reduces origin traffic at good hit rates, while edge egress still exists. Use byte hit rates for bandwidth planning: a 99% request hit rate does not imply 99% of bytes are cached if misses are huge objects. Upload/transcoding planning includes ingress and multiple renditions, not only originals.

4a. Cache: size the working set, not every read

A cache retains entries, not a new copy for every read. A million reads of one key may require only one entry. Estimate distinct retained keys over the reuse window, value sizes and key, metadata and allocator overhead, then apply the chosen replication and headroom. TTLs, eviction and access distribution determine the actual working set.

For illustration, two million distinct keys × 1 KB/value gives 2 GB of payload. With 50% extra for keys, metadata and allocation, one copy needs about 3 GB; two copies need about 6 GB in aggregate before headroom. This is cluster-wide memory, not 6 GB per node. Replica layout, shard skew, hot keys and safe utilization still determine node counts.

Caching 20% of the dataset does not establish a hit ratio. Measure reuse distance, access frequency and TTLs; a one-pass scan may have few hits despite a large cache. On cache loss or cold start, protect the origin with admission, request coalescing and controlled warm-up. A capacity-critical cache still needs a safe degradation policy.

5. Concurrency and Little’s Law

Little’s Law: keep the measurement scope consistent

  1. Choose the boundary

    For example: requests inside the API.

  2. Measure arrivals and time

    λ requests/s; W seconds/request.

  3. Infer mean in-flight work

    L requests = λ × W.

L = λ × W for a stable system, using means over the same boundary. Peak arrival rate multiplied by p99 latency is not Little’s Law.

In a stable system, L = λ × W: mean work in the system equals mean throughput times mean time in the system. At 1,000 requests/s and 0.2 s, the mean is 200 in-flight requests. The chosen boundary matters: if W includes queues, L includes queued requests. Substituting p99 latency does not produce mean concurrency.

Long-lived connections differ from active work: 50,000 WebSockets may carry only 100 messages/s while consuming sockets, buffers, heartbeats and file descriptors. Database connection pools should not match all HTTP connections; bound active queries by database capacity and use deadline-aware queues/backpressure.

6. Backlog and post-burst recovery

A queue absorbs temporary imbalance. With 2,000 jobs/s arriving and 1,500/s capacity for 60 seconds, backlog grows by 30,000 jobs. If arrivals later fall to 1,000/s, the 500/s surplus drains it in about 60 seconds before retries and variance. At 1,500/s arrivals, the backlog does not shrink.

Measure oldest-job age and deadline misses, not only queue length. Ten thousand short jobs and ten thousand video-encoding jobs represent very different work. A slow job blocking a partition or ordered key invalidates recovery estimates based only on total worker count; inspect key skew and concurrency.

7. Headroom for node loss and hot keys

Three nodes benchmarked at 500 requests/s each should not accept 1,500/s if one-node failure must be tolerated. After failure, only 1,000/s remains before recovery overhead; operate below that to preserve latency and rebalancing budget. Autoscaling has warm-up time and cannot rescue every seconds-long burst.

Hash partitioning spreads keys, not necessarily traffic. A hot tenant or viral URL can saturate one shard while others are idle. Estimate high-percentile per-key rates, read fan-out, cacheability and key-splitting options. Adding shards helps only if the load can move to them.

To tolerate losing one of three equal-capacity AZs, the surviving two must serve the full peak without waiting for autoscaling. At an illustrative one-million-request/s peak and a measured 10,000 requests/s/node under the chosen SLO, at least 100 nodes must survive: 50 per AZ, or 150 total with even routing. This is only an arithmetic minimum. Add recovery, skew and rollout allowance if absent from the benchmark, and validate databases and networks under AZ loss as well.

8. Vary assumptions and test sensitivity

The worksheet uses a basic payload formula. Inputs should represent one operation type; calculate reads/writes separately when their payloads differ. Storage excludes indexes/replicas/headroom; add those in your assumption sheet instead of treating the result as a final cluster size.

8a. Latency: optimize the critical path

Orders of magnitude matter more than memorizing one hardware table: CPU caches are typically faster than RAM, which is faster than storage/network round trips, while geographic distance adds propagation limits. Actual latency depends on hardware, payloads, queues and locality. Use workload measurements instead of presenting illustrations as current benchmarks.

For branched requests, wall time follows the critical path while resource work includes every branch. Parallelism can lower latency while increasing concurrency and tail amplification when all dependencies must finish. The percentile of a sum is not generally the sum of percentiles without distribution/correlation assumptions; measure end-to-end latency and trace causes.

8b. Availability and error budgets

For a time-based SLI, 99.9% over 30 days permits 0.1% × 30 × 24 × 60 = 43.2 minutes unavailable. At 99.99% this is 4.32 minutes, and 99.999% gives 25.92 seconds. Nines do not describe individual outages, blast radius or data loss; choose an SLI matching user experience.

Multiplying serial-dependency availability requires appropriate independence and success assumptions; shared failure domains often violate them. Redundancy does not create independence when replicas share regions, deployment bugs or revoked credentials. Error budgets guide reliability/change priorities, not permission to ignore incidents until the budget is exhausted.

For eight required dependencies, each independently successful with probability 99.9% at the selected boundary, all eight succeed with probability 0.999^8 ≈ 99.203%, not 99.9%. Optional dependencies or correlated failures require a different model. Multiplying vendor SLAs is not an end-to-end measurement; measure the user-facing SLI and test graceful degradation.

8b.1. Explore dependency chains, redundancy and common causes

This tool compares two separate structures: a serial chain requiring every dependency and a redundant group needing one available replica. Enable the common cause to see why replicas cannot escape a shared failure domain. This is a probability model with conditional independence assumptions, excluding failover capacity, detection, health checks, routing and data loss. Designs and SLI measurements still need those factors.

Tool percentages are rounded to five decimal places. A displayed 100.00000% after rounding does not prove zero failure probability; rounded values do not establish an SLA or durability guarantee.

8c. Worked example: from a photo app to design decisions

Read-path decision

  • Separate metadata APIs from media delivery and measure each path.
  • Evaluate edge delivery by geography and byte hits while allowing origin miss spikes.

Storage decision

  • Keep image objects in object storage and metadata/permissions in a database.
  • Compare retention, lifecycle and redundancy against durability and repair cost; 225 TB is not a finalized disk allocation.

These are exercise assumptions, not product measurements: twenty million DAU, twenty-five photo views and 0.05 uploads per active user per day. Each upload retains a 2 MB original plus 0.5 MB of variants. Each view transfers one 200 KB image. Use decimal units, thirty-day retention and three copies, initially excluding metadata, further compression, protocol overhead and retries.

Distinguish photo deliveries from feed API calls: one API call can return several photos, so the photo-view rate is not the feed API rate. Illustrative peak factors of five for views and three for uploads apply to those rates. Storage uses actual daily uploads; do not multiply an entire day by a peak factor.

At a 95% CDN byte hit ratio for this workload, the origin serves about 5 TB/day, averaging 57.9 MB/s, while viewer delivery remains 100 TB/day. These are average baselines: cold starts, viral objects, invalidation and failover can produce much larger miss peaks. Double payload size or view count to see which resources and costs grow.

9. Worked exercises

Does ten million DAU mean ten million concurrent requests?

No. DAU counts users active during a day. RPS/concurrency require daily operations and processing time or session activity. Twenty requests/user/day gives about 2,315 average requests/s before peak and residence-time analysis.

Exercise: 800 jobs/s arrive, workers serve 600/s for 90 seconds; arrivals then fall to 400/s. How long to drain?

Backlog grows by (800−600) × 90 = 18,000 jobs. The post-burst surplus is 600−400 = 200 jobs/s, so draining takes about 90 seconds. Recompute using effective throughput if retries or a slow downstream reduce service rate.

Why can average payloads not guarantee every request fits in memory?

Averages estimate totals but hide tails. A 100 MB request can exhaust buffers despite a 10 KB mean. Use size limits, streaming/chunking, concurrency budgets and payload distributions to protect individual requests and aggregate resources.

Do more decimal places make an estimate more accurate?

Not when assumptions are uncertain. Round by order of magnitude, state ranges and test sensitivity. A peak-factor error of two matters more than a 1% rounding error; benchmarks and telemetry reduce actual uncertainty.

Open questions: explain the underlying mechanisms

Attempt an answer before reading, then change an assumption and reconsider the conclusion. Answers retain units and failure boundaries; the preceding sections provide diagrams and models.

What capacity follows from 50 million DAU and twenty daily requests each?

The daily total is one billion requests, or about 11574 requests/s on average at the chosen boundary. DAU does not determine concurrency or peaks. Derive peaks from traffic distribution, bursts, time zones, events and growth. A provisional 4× factor gives roughly 46300 edge requests/s, explicitly as an assumption rather than a measurement.

Split the operation mix next: ten percent writes with one hundred downstream events per write can make queue and sink capacity dominate edge RPS. Size using measured per-node capacity under relevant latency/error targets, accounting for failures, retries and rollouts. A universal QPS-per-server number ignores payloads, storage and hardware.

Which quantities belong in a retained-photo storage estimate?

Start with uploads/day times average stored bytes per upload times retained days. Ten million daily photos at 2 MB decimal for 1825 days give about 36.5 PB logically; this illustration uses 365 days per year. State how average sizes were obtained and whether variants are included. Three-way replication makes these bytes 109.5 PB before overhead.

Account for metadata, indexes, versions, incomplete uploads, redundancy layout, repair or collection workspace and headroom. Age-based retention, holds and derived copies may differ. Disk bytes are only one constraint: model uploads, read amplification, egress, repairs and growth. Erasure coding changes redundancy overhead without removing these costs.

What does one million viewers at five Mbps imply for bandwidth?

One million simultaneous viewers averaging five megabits/s require five terabits/s of viewer egress, or 625 decimal gigabytes/s before overhead and retries. This is delivery bandwidth, not retained storage or necessarily origin traffic. An ideal 95% byte hit ratio would leave about 250 gigabits/s of origin media egress under otherwise compatible assumptions.

Consider delivery networks, regional placement, prefill and coalescing against geography, workload and costs rather than treating the number as a vendor mandate. Origin offload does not eliminate edge-to-viewer bytes. Startup bursts, bitrate ladders, live versus VOD behavior, correlated misses and failover can change peaks substantially.

How should binary anchors and latency estimates be used?

Powers of two help with bit budgets, address spaces and binary units:2^10=1024,2^20=1048576 and 2^30=1073741824. One KiB is 1024 bytes and one MiB is 2^20; decimal kB and GB use powers of 1000. A 64-symbol alphabet carries six bits per character, whereas 62 symbols carry about 5.954; uniqueness and collision handling remain separate concerns.

Latency anchors are conditional orders of magnitude rather than service guarantees. Memory, storage, cache, RPC and wide-area delays depend on hardware, contention, payload, routes and warm state. Give an assumed range and validate with the relevant workload; adding component p99 values does not establish the end-to-end p99.
