Building Netflix: evolve from Play to global streaming

Explore fourteen architectural stages with runnable diagrams, rate–quality candidates, CDN prefill and live join pressure, explaining authority, QoE and trade-offs at each step.

From one Play button to a system that learns from failure

  1. Play correctly

    Rights, sessions, manifests and licenses have explicit authority.

  2. Deliver efficiently

    Encoding evidence → placement/prefill → player QoE.

  3. Prove resilience

    Bounded dependencies, regional budgets and recovery drills.

  4. Extend the product

    Governed learning, ads and live with distinct contracts.

Understand why components are added, trace requests and test assumptions before choosing technology.

  • Control authorizes sessions, media delivers bytes and feedback measures quality and learns.

1. Build a streaming service through real pressures

Successful experience

  • Discover eligible titles, start promptly, play continuously with correct audio/subtitles and resume sensibly.

Explicit boundaries

  • Separate VOD/live and ad/no-ad; define devices, territories, rights, QoE targets and deadlines.

Earn complexity

  • Cloud, microservices, a custom CDN or active-active are useful when they address measured constraints.

This build uses Netflix as a product context for architectural evolution: VOD, discovery, multiple devices and regions, advertising and live. Components, contracts, capacity and labs are AV Lore teaching designs rather than the company’s current internal system. First read Video Platform for transcoding DAGs, segmented media, ABR and bandwidth; this chapter explains why and when layers are added and what they cost.

Public milestones provide orientation: streaming launched in 2007, a 2008 database corruption incident helped motivate a cloud migration publicly described as complete in 2016. A DVD-shipping disruption does not establish that all streaming stopped for three days. Stages below follow design questions; real initiatives overlap and are not a mandatory checklist for every startup.

2. Read the system through one title’s lifecycle

Four loops with different failures

  1. Prepare

    Master → validation → encodes → quality → publish.

  2. Authorize

    Identity → rights/device checks → session/manifest/license.

  3. Deliver

    Steering → CDN → buffer/ABR → decrypt/decode.

  4. Learn and recover

    QoE/history → governed data → experiments/drills.

Correlate evidence using sessions, title generations, devices and policy versions.

A title has an ingestion identity and source version. Validate video, audio and subtitles, encode candidates, choose a ladder, package/encrypt, quality-check and publish a generation. Manifests reference only ready objects. Rollouts and rollbacks preserve readable old generations under retention/rights policies rather than deleting assets immediately.

Playback has two paths: small metadata/authorization calls critical to startup, and large shareable CDN media traffic. Feedback is a third path for history, QoE, experiments and learning. Slow analytics should not block every segment, and data fresh enough for ranking is not automatically fresh enough for authorization.

3. 14 stages: add architecture for a reason

Select stages, inspect component responsibilities and run traces. Four panels distinguish pressures, decisions, trade-offs and reasons to move on. Read exit criteria before adding components: untested recovery may merely move failures somewhere harder to observe.

Start with working playback

The MVP has one application for sign-in, catalog and viewing rights. A small rendition set is encoded and served through a commercial CDN. Separate control from media bytes immediately: the application authorizes a session while the CDN handles segment requests. This separation does not require splitting every domain into microservices.

Problem pressure: Users need Play to start reliably on a small device set; a small team must be able to debug the whole request.

Architectural decision: Use a modular monolith, relational metadata and managed CDN. Publish assets before manifests and correlate startup, license and rebuffering failures with session IDs.

Costs and trade-offs: Few deployment units simplify delivery, but releases and database failures have wider impact. CDN integration and client instrumentation still require work.

When is the next step needed?: Move on when measured startup tails or database/release coupling obstruct goals.

Demonstrate end-to-end playback, correct rights, media bypassing app servers and tested metadata/manifest rollback.

The MVP still needs rights, DRM and monitoring; simplicity means fewer operated components.

Make recovery explicit

A critical database must not mean one unrecoverable copy. Separate failure domains and prepare replication, failover, immutable backups and restore drills. Replication improves availability but can replicate corruption or deletion; retained, verified backups provide a different recovery layer.

Problem pressure: A data or datacenter failure can disable control while real RPO/RTO remain unknown.

Architectural decision: Define authority, backup points and promotion fencing. Migrate domains with backfill, CDC, verification and explicit cutover/rollback checkpoints.

Costs and trade-offs: Dual writes can diverge without an outbox or transaction boundary; cloud hosting does not repair schema coupling or corrupt data.

When is the next step needed?: Recovery is measured, but teams still share release bottlenecks or competing query workloads.

Restore into a clean environment, validate invariants, measure loss and recovery time and fence the old writer.

SQL supports HA; choose engines by access patterns and consistency rather than a scale label.

Split by domain ownership

Split catalog, accounts, recommendations and playback when scale, releases or isolation justify it. Domains own contracts and data. Discovery locates healthy endpoints but does not establish retry safety. Avoid a hidden shared database dependency across dozens of services.

Problem pressure: Shared schemas and releases couple teams; recommendation workloads differ from entitlement lookups.

Architectural decision: Establish APIs, service identity, discovery, health and per-hop timeouts; propagate changes with events instead of forcing every read through remote joins.

Costs and trade-offs: Distributed tracing, schema compatibility, eventual projections and on-call ownership add cost. Excessive synchronous fan-out worsens p99.

When is the next step needed?: Delivery cost and quality dominate application compute, motivating placement near viewer networks.

Each service has an owner, SLO, versioned contract, capacity bound and reviewed fallback, with no unbounded call cycles.

Boundaries help when ownership and failure behavior are explicit; discovery is only one part.

Place media near ISPs

At stable volume with network partners, consider embedded caches or IXP placement alongside commercial CDN. Dedicated delivery infrastructure is one option, but no universal subscriber threshold mandates building a CDN. Optimize QoE and total cost by geography/network rather than only aggregate hit rates.

Problem pressure: Transit distance, cost and peak congestion impair playback even when APIs are fast.

Architectural decision: Forecast regional popularity, prefill off peak and publish complete assets; steering considers availability, locality, health and capacity.

Costs and trade-offs: Hardware, ISP agreements, fill bandwidth, forecast error and fallback capacity need operations. Commercial CDNs may also preposition assets.

When is the next step needed?: Hot titles or cache failures create miss storms; dependency degradation requires containment.

Measure byte and request hits separately, prevent steering to absent generations and test cache loss and fill deadlines.

CDN placement does not move entitlement authority into media caches.

Contain dependency failures

The home page may fall back to popular titles when recommendations are slow; Play cannot bypass rights or issue unrestricted licenses. Deadlines, bounded jittered retries, breakers and bulkheads protect resources. Chaos requires observability, hypotheses, abort thresholds and recovery capability.

Problem pressure: Recommendation timeouts exhaust workers and drag playback down.

Architectural decision: Separate optional and critical concurrency pools, propagate deadlines and filter fallback content by rights/maturity.

Costs and trade-offs: Fallbacks lose freshness/personalization; oversensitive breakers and retry amplification create failures. Measure degraded experience too.

When is the next step needed?: Devices make too many calls or receive excessive payloads, motivating adapters without duplicating authorization.

Boundedly inject latency; preserve playback resources, shed optional work and verify rights never fail open.

Degrading one feature does not waive another feature’s correctness.

Adapt by device

TVs, browsers and mobile clients differ in input, screens, memory, codec/DRM and upgrade cadence. Device APIs assemble compact view models while domain services retain business rules. Titles may have different playable packages; cache identities must include response-relevant dimensions without sharing personalized responses across users.

Problem pressure: Excessive round trips and new metadata break clients with long upgrade lifetimes.

Architectural decision: Use capability negotiation, versioned contracts and bounded server-driven layouts, progressively rolled out by device cohort.

Costs and trade-offs: BFF duplication and cache cardinality grow; aggregation or GraphQL does not automatically bound fan-out.

When is the next step needed?: Regional loss must preserve device access and rights/session semantics.

Test the compatibility matrix, old-client fallbacks and personalized-cache isolation; observe startup by app, codec and DRM.

Views may differ while rights remain one consistent domain.

Design multiple regions

Multiple regions tolerate regional loss but do not make state automatically consistent. Classify state: immutable media and catalog projections differ from account restrictions or concurrent-stream leases. Define partition behavior: reject, route to a home region or use explicitly accepted bounded staleness.

Problem pressure: One region cannot meet global availability or latency goals.

Architectural decision: Route control traffic to ready regions, replicate under domain contracts and verify survivor capacity; media routing remains separate.

Costs and trade-offs: Lag, egress, cold caches and split-brain risks increase. Warm, sufficiently provisioned and drilled active-passive designs can also work.

When is the next step needed?: Healthy dashboards do not prove failover; exercise controlled real traffic transfers.

After losing a region, admitted load remains supportable, write/rights authority is unambiguous and lag/RPO are visible.

Region count is not failure tolerance when survivors have no spare capacity.

Prove evacuation with drills

Gradually evacuate a region against a clear hypothesis: surviving regions sustain X requests/second within startup p99 targets. Test DNS, persistent connections, warmup, retries, queues and recovery backlog. Logical diagrams express intent; drills reveal overlooked dependencies.

Problem pressure: Failover exists only in a runbook; operator and automation effectiveness is unknown.

Architectural decision: Run game days with load bounds, abort triggers, dry runs, rollback owners and evidence by client, region and ISP.

Costs and trade-offs: Drills consume reserves and carry real risk; start with controlled hypotheses rather than random production destruction.

When is the next step needed?: Growing QoE and behavioral events need a learning pipeline separate from control authority.

Record before/during/after results, convergence, resource peaks, rollback and verified remediation.

Measure recovery and tail experience; server 200 rates are insufficient.

Build the feedback pipeline

Playback telemetry, clicks and history have distinct purposes, retention and privacy. Durable logs enable replay; processors deduplicate by event/session sequence and handle event-time lateness. Warehouses provide analytical projections, not entitlement or billing authority.

Problem pressure: Events outgrow transactional traffic; teams need QoE, experiments and recommendation freshness.

Architectural decision: Version schemas, minimize personal data, enforce consent/deletion and use replay-safe sinks; separate fast QoE streams from batch analytics.

Costs and trade-offs: At-least-once delivery needs bounded deduplication; schema evolution, backfills and deletion propagation are ongoing work.

When is the next step needed?: New countries require rights, localization, time zones and device readiness as well as bandwidth.

Drops, duplicates and late events do not silently inflate metrics; history freshness and dataset permissions are explicit.

The pipeline is feedback, not a mandatory dependency for every segment.

Prepare global launches

Catalog presence does not establish worldwide viewing rights. Territory windows, maturity, accounts and time need authoritative policy. Subtitles, audio and artwork need generations and quality checks. Launch readiness covers capacity, device/codec/DRM support and customer-facing failure behavior.

Problem pressure: New traffic brings different geographies, languages and rights; clocks and stale caches can authorize incorrectly.

Architectural decision: Use policy-versioned availability, localized manifests and geographic/device rollouts; bound cached rights by enforcement requirements.

Costs and trade-offs: Rights invalidation and clock boundaries are difficult; preferred regions do not determine legal territory and VPN detection is imperfect.

When is the next step needed?: A growing catalog makes uniform encoding wasteful for simple content or inadequate for difficult content.

Test rights expiry, time boundaries, audio/subtitle synchronization and devices; roll back one title without taking down the catalog.

Global rollout is a compatibility and policy exercise, not just more instances.

Optimize by title and shot

Measure encoding candidates instead of assigning one bitrate per resolution to every film. Clean animation may reach targets with fewer bits while grain/action needs more. Low-rate 1080p can sometimes outperform higher-rate 720p, but this must be measured per content/device. Per-shot optimization adds granularity and segment/keyframe alignment requirements.

Problem pressure: Fixed ladders waste delivery/storage while producing uneven quality.

Architectural decision: Use rate–distortion evidence, device/codec constraints and ABR-compatible ladders, with difficult-scene quality gates before publication.

Costs and trade-offs: More encodes, metrics and codec variants cost compute and QA; metrics do not replace human review or device compatibility.

When is the next step needed?: Efficient delivery still needs effective discovery and guarded experiments.

Compare at declared perceptual targets by device cohort; validate segment boundaries, captions, DRM and rollback generations.

A frontier is meaningful only for the measured codec, metric and content.

Learn discovery through experiments

Ranking, artwork and row composition influence viewing. Experiment assignment must be stable for its unit. Offline ranking quality does not prove causal lift; online tests need startup, rebuffering, latency, diversity and other guardrails alongside engagement. No toy formula is the company’s real algorithm.

Problem pressure: Large catalogs are hard to discover; an offline-improved model may be too slow or narrow the experience.

Architectural decision: Combine offline pipelines with bounded online serving, filter eligible candidates, assign experiments and log actual exposure.

Costs and trade-offs: Feedback bias, leakage, missing exposure and cross-device assignment undermine conclusions; fallback needs accurate experiment logging too.

When is the next step needed?: Ads add latency-sensitive decisions and measurement that must not be confused with playback truth.

Maintain holdouts, guardrails, reproducible assignment/model versions and policy-compliant fallbacks.

A click or request alone is neither rendered exposure nor causal improvement.

Add deadline-bound advertising

An ad tier needs consent, eligibility, frequency controls and decision deadlines. Break planning and stitching preserve media timelines and rights. Impression signals need identities, reconciliation and fraud checks. A successful decision is not a viewed ad; timeout fallback depends on policy/contracts rather than always skipping ads.

Problem pressure: Slow ad calls or creatives must not create unbounded startup stalls or corrupt timelines.

Architectural decision: Budget decisions within playback deadlines, prevalidate creatives and separate measurement from segment serving.

Costs and trade-offs: Personalization reduces manifest cacheability; frequency/business state has consistency and privacy requirements.

When is the next step needed?: Live adds simultaneous joins, new-segment misses and source failures beyond what VOD prefill solves.

Test no-fill, timeouts, continuity, consent and impression deduplication; define billable-event authority.

Extend to live streaming

Live still benefits from CDN sharing, but future media cannot be prefilled. Prewarm capacity and known initialization assets; shield/coalesce origins when new segments appear. Redundant ingest and encoders require aligned timelines, keyframes, codec configuration and discontinuity handling for playable failover.

Problem pressure: Millions join together, request the same cold segment and depend on fallible live sources.

Architectural decision: Separate join admission from shared-media fan-out; use redundant contribution, encoders/origins and tested player recovery.

Costs and trade-offs: Larger buffers improve resilience but increase glass-to-glass latency; low-latency chunks raise request pressure and reduce outage coverage.

When is the next step needed?: Continue rehearsals and cohort measurement; two encoders alone do not establish reliability.

Rehearse peak joins, origin loss, source failover and recovery backlog; test caption/audio/DRM continuity and fallback on real devices.

Segment sharing reduces origin work, not bytes ultimately delivered to viewers.

4. Estimate four different budgets

Assume twenty million daily active users, five percent concurrently watching at peak and five Mbps average video. A million concurrent viewers require roughly five Tbps of edge video payload, before audio, overhead, retries and geography. Do not proxy this through application servers. At steady concurrency with thirty-minute average sessions, arrivals are approximately 1,000,000 / 1,800 ≈ 556 starts/second. This steady-state reasoning does not describe synchronized live-event joins.

Four-second segments produce about 250 thousand viewer-edge video requests/second for a million viewers, excluding audio, subtitles and manifests. Separate byte and request hits: at 98 percent byte hits, five Tbps implies around 100 Gbps of miss payload before overhead, tiering or coalescing. Request hits cannot substitute for byte hits when object sizes differ.

One hour at five Mbps is 2.25 decimal GB. Catalog storage sums all renditions, codecs and audio, multiplied by durations, replication and retained generations. Delivery follows renditions viewers actually choose, not the entire ladder per view. Delivery, storage, encoding, caches, idle reserves and engineering need contract-specific cost models rather than compute counts alone.

5. Trace Play, licenses, media and feedback

Startup traces distinguish authorization from media transport. Clients may overlap license and media work where DRM and packaging allow; the diagram shows logical dependencies, not a mandatory sequential schedule. Cached segments do not remove valid-session/license requirements. Encrypted assets can share cache scopes while tokens and logs still need protection.

The feedback trace distinguishes acknowledged progress, durable intake and applied history. At-least-once intake needs stable identity and bounded deduplication contracts. Concurrent devices require session/version policies; choosing the largest playback position or client timestamp blindly is not sufficient.

Where does one Play action go?

Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

The control plane authorizes sessions and selects media; the data plane delivers encrypted segments. Traces are an educational design, not an assertion of current private topology.

Start playback

  1. Play sends title/profile/capabilities and a stable request ID.
  2. Authenticate/admit and propagate deadlines; create a retry-safe session under its contract.
  3. Validate entitlement, territory/windows, maturity and stream-limit policy.
  4. Select a generation/device package and healthy CDN candidates with inventory.
  5. Return session IDs, manifests, license routes and token expiry.

License and media

  1. Send a scoped DRM challenge without logging secrets into telemetry.
  2. Validate under rights/key policy; timeouts never fail open.
  3. Return licenses under device/scheme policy and appropriate key security boundaries.
  4. Fetch manifests/segments with scoped authorization and correct cache identity.
  5. Misses use shielding/coalescing rather than routing every media byte through account APIs.
  6. The player decrypts/decodes, selects renditions from throughput/buffers and reports QoE.

History and learning

  1. Send sequenced event-time QoE/progress under consent bounds.
  2. Deduplicate, handle lateness and upsert progress under session/version contracts.
  3. Update governed features/projections with freshness metadata.
  4. Return policy-compliant catalog/ranking with bounded fallback.

Publish VOD and prefill

  1. Complete encoding, QA and packaging; publish assets before manifests.
  2. Prefill selected complete assets off peak and publish inventory after validation.
  3. Report health, inventory and capacity; stale health is not proof of availability.
  4. Return only candidates compatible with generation and device/rights context.

Live and degraded paths

  1. Redundant live ingest creates aligned generations without future prefill.
  2. Many viewers request the same new segment; edges coalesce shared keys.
  3. Shields collapse misses; failures use tested alternate-generation/continuity policies.
  4. On candidate failure, bounded refresh/failover follows session/token expiry.
  5. Report startup, rebuffering and live latency to observe degraded UX beyond server 200 rates.

6. APIs and state: small, explicit and retry-safe

These are illustrative APIs. Request IDs help only when servers retain results/ownership under scoped idempotency and retention; they do not make POST exactly-once. Sessions pin principals/profiles, title generations, device/DRM capabilities, policy versions, expiry and state. Define renewal behavior to avoid synchronized token refresh during playback.

Do not place raw license keys or bearer tokens in warehouses. Minimize event personal fields; TTLs, access logs and deletion policies cover caches, projections and backups. Avoid unnecessary identity in media URLs and logs. Cache authorization must enforce scope; shareable encrypted bytes do not imply public entitlement.

POST /v1/playback-sessions

Authorize Play and return a client contract.

{"requestId":"p-781","titleId":"t42","profileId":"pr7","device":{"codec":["avc"],"drm":"supported-scheme"}}
201 { sessionId, titleGeneration, manifestUrl, licenseEndpoint, mediaCandidates, expiresAt, policyVersion }

  • Rights denials cannot be retried by bypassing policy; 429/503 use bounded retries and contract-defined Retry-After.

7. Startup budgets and dependency isolation

Controlled degradation

  • Optional artwork, row ordering or ranking can fall back to filtered metadata/popular candidates.

Preserve invariants

  • Rights, maturity, account restrictions, licenses and plans must not be bypassed on timeout.

Observe clients

  • Measure first-frame time, rebuffer ratios/durations, switches, failures and live latency by cohort.

Define startup from Play to first frame, segmented by cold/warm app, DRM, geography and live/VOD. Fast API p99 does not guarantee first frames: DNS/TLS, app startup, authorization, manifests, licenses, buffers and decode all contribute. Summing hop p99s is not an end-to-end p99 estimate; use correlated client and distributed timings to find critical paths.

Propagate parent deadlines and remaining budgets. Retry only safe operations within bounded budgets. Isolate browse/recommendation resources from Play/license resources. Slow decorations may fall back to simple eligible layouts. An unavailable entitlement authority must follow freshness/error policy rather than grant access. Stale cached grants are also decisions about revocation.

8. Per-title encoding: quality needs evidence

A fixed resolution/bitrate ladder is simple but ignores content compressibility. Per-title ladders use measured rate–quality curves; per-shot processing allocates bits at finer granularity. This is not a complexity multiplier. Codec, metric, crop, grain treatment and device changes require renewed measurement and validation.

A Pareto frontier contains candidates not dominated by another with no greater bitrate and no lower quality, with at least one strict improvement. Real convex-hull and ladder optimization adds constraints; the lab only shows nondominated candidates and minimum-rate target choices. Apply device caps before computing frontiers: excluding 1080p can make a previously dominated 720p point useful.

Preserve switching-compatible segment boundaries, keyframes and representation metadata. A new codec may save bytes for a cohort while adding encoding, storage, compatibility, energy and rollout costs. Preserve supported legacy codecs and rollback generations. Compare quality, failures and cost by cohort instead of asserting one saving for every film.

9. CDN prefill, inventory and steering

Prefill shifts some peak demand off peak when caches contain the correct renditions and generations. Title-level forecasts alone miss regional codec, audio and device variation. Request hits count requests while byte hits count bytes. Rare large objects and early abandonment can make these measures diverge; starts times full-file size is not universal demand.

In the lab, A has the most predicted plays but costs six GB. Choosing A first can displace B+C, whose avoided bytes are higher within ten GB. This is a 0/1 knapsack constrained by storage and fill windows. Full-file views, complete availability and accurate forecasts are assumptions. Production uses chunks, shifting demand, tiers and disk/network constraints; tail QoE or fairness may matter more than bytes.

Steering combines inventory freshness, health, locality and capacity. A healthy server without a title is insufficient. Return bounded candidates with session/token and failover policies. Shields and coalescing reduce synchronized misses for shared objects, not every personalized request. Eviction and invalidation preserve generations. Test appliance failure, stale inventory and new-title spikes separately.

10. Multi-region: capacity, state and drills

Assume three regions each sustain at most 100K control requests/second. A total peak of 180K gives 60K each; after one loss the survivors average 90K, close to ceilings before retries and warmup. At 240K total, survivors need 120K each, exceeding capacity despite pre-failure 80 percent utilization. Current single-region utilization does not establish evacuation readiness.

Immutable media are easier to replicate than restrictions and concurrent-stream counters. Choose home-region authority, globally consistent storage or an explicit bounded-stale policy, defining unreachable-authority behavior. Eventual history may be acceptable with merge rules while newly revoked access may not be. Active-active describes traffic topology, not consistency guarantees.

Game days start with hypotheses and bounded shifts. Observe client startup/rebuffering, rights errors, queue lag, dependencies, DNS/connections and retries. Predefine abort thresholds, owners, automation, rollback and recovery checks. Catch-up and reconciliation continue after failover; returning traffic too quickly can create another miss/retry storm.

11. Measure discovery and feedback correctly

Exposure

  • The treatment is actually rendered to the unit with assignment/model versions and consent.

QoE

  • First frames, rebuffering and device/network errors, not just aggregate engagement.

Privacy

  • Minimize fields and govern access, retention, deletion and feature lineage.

Discovery includes retrieval, eligibility, ranking and composition. Artwork and localization need asset versions; features need freshness and consent. Browse filtering improves experience while Play still authorizes current policy. Slow recommendation dependencies can use eligible popular/current catalog fallbacks with separate degraded-path metrics.

Experiments require stable assignment units, actual rendered exposure, model/version identities and guardrails. Assignment without rendering is not exposure. Offline ranking/click metrics do not prove user benefit; novelty, bias, repeated measures and latency matter. Prespecify success criteria, inspect segments and uncertainty and avoid declaring lift from one average.

QoE events need IDs, session sequences, event time and schema versions, handling skew, lateness, duplicates and offline clients. Retention/deletion policies cover features, projections and exports. Operational dashboards and analytical warehouses have different latency; compare metrics only under compatible denominators, windows and definitions.

12. Ads: deadlines, timelines and measurement

Ad-supported playback adds tier, consent, frequency context and decision services. Personalized manifests and stitched media preserve codec, audio, timeline and DRM continuity on supported devices. Bound decisions within startup deadlines. Timeout/no-fill behavior is a product/contract policy, potentially fallback creatives or another action, never an assumed universal skip.

Define requests, decisions, impressions, progress and billable events separately. A decision-service 200 proves neither creative download nor visibility. Stable identities, reconciliation, bounded deduplication, fraud and privacy controls preserve meaning. Slow measurement can buffer/retry under policy without synchronous warehouse writes for each segment.

Shared segments can remain cacheable while manifests are personalized, with distinct keys, scopes and token validation. Cross-device/region frequency caps have separate consistency/freshness trade-offs. Roll out by cohort, rehearse no-fill and creative mismatch and retain tier/device QoE guardrails.

13. Live: join surges, origins and buffers

VOD can preposition media before peaks; live media exists only after ingest/encoding. Prewarm infrastructure, capacity, connections and known initialization assets, but not future frames. Viewers near the same live edge share segments, so CDN remains useful. Edge coalescing can reduce per-viewer fetches to per-POP/variant fetches; shielding can reduce further under compatible key, timing, cacheability and generation assumptions.

The control plane still handles authentication, licenses and startup. Three million viewers joining in thirty seconds with two calls each create about 200K control requests/second even with perfect media hits. Admission, queues, established sessions, renewal jitter and overload UX need separate planning; licenses and grants cannot be coalesced by media keys.

Redundant encoders may differ in timestamps, keyframes and codec configuration. Failover needs generation, sequence and discontinuity contracts plus device tests. Buffers hide short outages at the cost of latency. Low-latency chunks change requests, availability and budgets; this lab uses full four-second segments to expose a trade-off, not predict low-latency performance.

16. Practice explanations and challenges

Explain scope, sizing, one Play trace, bottlenecks, evolution and failure evidence. Choose two deep dives instead of listing components: for example per-title evidence and join surges, or rights consistency and placement. Answers below reason from this chapter’s assumptions; different constraints may justify different designs.

Why can first frames be slow despite fast APIs?

APIs are only part of the path. Inspect cold app, DNS/TLS, rights, manifests, licenses, buffering and decode in client traces by cohort. Tail delays may correlate, so summed hop p99s are not end-to-end p99. Measure from Play to first frame beyond server logs.

Does encrypted CDN media mean anyone may watch?

No. Cacheable bytes and rights to sessions/licenses are separate contracts. Validate scoped access, devices/DRM and fresh rights; protect tokens/logs. Operational metadata can still relate to viewers.

Does per-title always save fifty percent at equal quality?

There is no universal percentage. Measure curves by content/codec/device and target, validate quality and alignment and account for encoding, storage and delivery. Synthetic lab candidates above a common target are not identical quality or a production BD-rate result.

Why can popular A lose to B+C in prefill?

A uses six of ten GB, leaving D+E and 770 avoided GB in the fixture. B+C uses ten GB and avoids 1,000 GB. The objective is bytes under whole-file and fill constraints; starts, partial viewing, latency or forecast changes alter the optimum.

Can three regions tolerate losing one?

Region count is insufficient. Check survivor dependency capacity, caches, retries, queues, routing and authority. Three 100K/s regions at 240K total need 120K each after a loss and overload. Test client QoE in drills rather than counting instances.

If future live media cannot be prefilled, does CDN still help?

Yes. Viewers share published segments; caches, coalescing and shields reduce repeated origin work and improve locality. Prewarm capacity and known assets. Personalized startup traffic needs separate capacity and shared delivery still sends bytes to every viewer.

Do two live encoders ensure seamless failover?

No. Timelines, timestamps, keyframes, codecs, sequences and discontinuities need compatibility; contribution paths and origins need independent failure domains. Buffers hide some outages but add latency. Rehearse device behavior and source/origin faults.

Does an ad-decision 200 establish an impression?

No. Distinguish decisions, delivered media, rendered impressions and billable events. Stable IDs, deduplication, reconciliation, consent and fraud controls define measurement. No-fill/timeout behavior follows contracts rather than an assumed skip.
