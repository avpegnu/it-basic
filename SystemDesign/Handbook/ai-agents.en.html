Agents & Tool Use: loops, authority and durable execution

Choose workflows or agents, design tool contracts, manage approvals/budgets/context and recover external effects correctly. Explore runtime traces, lost-response recovery and input-token growth.

Execute a task under explicit control

  1. Scope tasks

    Define principals, scope and outcome criteria.

  2. Propose

    Propose actions from bounded context.

  3. Validate / admit

    Check schemas, authority, budgets, preconditions and approvals.

  4. Execute / record

    Bind stable intents, external receipts and durable observations.

  5. Continue / stop

    Verify outcomes, bound loops and escalate or reconcile.

Do not collapse proposals, authorization, approval and committed effects into one tool-success state.

  • Agent autonomy selects control flow; runtimes and resource servers enforce authority and business constraints.
  • Use bounded workflows when suitable and add model-directed steps for measured task needs rather than labels.
  • Tool proposals and outputs are untrusted data; schema validity does not establish authority or correctness.
  • Durable checkpoints do not atomically commit external APIs; stable intents and receipts/reconciliation address crash windows.
  • Budgets and cancellation stop future work without undoing committed effects or incurred costs.
  • Context growth, caching, external memory and lossy summaries have distinct assumptions and quality trade-offs.

1. Choose workflows or agents by control flow and evidence

Workflow

  • Code defines sequences, branches and transitions.
  • Clear task structure can simplify contract testing.

Bounded agent

  • Models propose actions within allowed spaces.
  • Require trajectory evaluation, budgets and recovery.

Hybrid

  • Combine exploration with deterministic product boundaries.
  • For example, flexible research with code-enforced refunds.

Workflows use code to choose known steps and branches, with models at selected nodes. Agents allow models to select actions, tools or subtasks within runtime bounds. Systems can combine them: ticket workflows may include agentic research before deterministic approval/payment rules. Multiple model calls alone do not establish autonomy.

Compare against simpler baselines on quality, reliability, costs and operational complexity. Use autonomy when dynamic planning/search provides measured value; known-step tasks can often be bounded more easily in workflows. Agents do not solve arbitrary tasks, and poorly built workflows can also be unsafe or unbounded.

2. Runtime loops and completion contracts

From proposals to verified observations

  1. Build context

    Assemble goals, evidence, schemas and limits.

  2. Model proposal

    Receive text or tool names/arguments.

  3. Validate / reserve

    Check authority, preconditions, budgets and approval.

  4. Execute / observe

    Bound results and record receipts/errors.

Return tool outputs as data rather than new authority.

A typical cycle assembles context, obtains proposals, validates/admit actions, executes tools and records observations before continuing or stopping. ReAct is one reasoning/action pattern rather than a requirement to expose private reasoning or use a single loop. Parallel tools, graphs and asynchronous waits still need explicit state transitions.

Model completion text is not evidence of task completion. Verify authoritative states, receipts or relevant tests. Distinguish completion, waiting, exhaustion, cancellation and unresolved outcomes, and keep errors structurally distinct from successful observations.

3. Tools are semantic APIs, not just names and schemas

Define tool purpose, types/bounds, outputs, identities, errors, pagination, timeouts, cost limits and effects. Prefer narrow domain operations where unrestricted interpreters are unnecessary. Descriptions guide selection, while executors and resource servers enforce constraints. Structured JSON does not prevent wrong resources or unauthorized actions.

Reads still require authorization, privacy, quotas and freshness controls; searches can disclose data without writing it. Writes and destructive/external actions have task-specific semantics and severity. Do not trust self-declared read-only annotations; review implementation and credentials, bound outputs and avoid secret-bearing errors.

4. Authority, business rules and prompt injection

Models cannot grant principals, tenants, spending limits or roles. Derive identity from trusted sessions, enforce server-side object authorization and inspect current business state. Ticket instructions cannot raise authorized limits. Separate data from authority, restrict capabilities and test injection scenarios; a prompt warning alone is not a security boundary.

Observations may contain malicious instructions, stale facts or forged approvals. Only trusted decision services establish consent; document text is not approval. Scope and revoke credentials by task/run/resource, and never let caller-provided tenant fields expand authority.

5. Bind approvals to concrete, valid actions

When review is required, present concrete targets, amounts/content changes, consequences and preconditions. Bind decisions to identities, canonical payload hashes, resource versions, validity and scope. A persistent global approval flag is inadequate; changed payloads or targets require appropriate revalidation.

Approval does not replace authorization or current business rules. Waiting can change resources, privileges and quotas, requiring revalidation before dispatch. Policies may preauthorize bounded low-risk actions rather than prompting for every operation; delegation, concrete risk review and revocation must remain explicit.

If budget expires after a write, the run can be unfinished with one committed effect; non-completion must not be treated as no effect.

7. Runtime, policy, checkpoints and external systems

Coordinators own run transitions without owning external transactions. Policies/executors enforce capabilities and effects, checkpoints preserve resumable evidence and resource servers own authoritative outcomes. Graph connections do not make writes atomic; recovery traces expose the commit/checkpoint gap.

Models propose; runtimes control execution

Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

Trace reads, write approvals, lost-response recovery, recall and cancellation. Authority, durable intent and external effects remain separate from model output.

An authorized read

  1. Bind tasks/principals to authorized tools and data.
  2. Build provenance-aware bounded contexts and tool descriptions.
  3. Validate proposals without treating model assertions as authorization.
  4. Scope credentials and bound time/output.
  5. Query permitted resources with explicit freshness metadata.
  6. Return observations as data rather than new authority.

Intent and approval before effects

  1. Check arguments, business limits and resource versions.
  2. Persist intent identity/payload hashes and await the store contract’s durable acknowledgement before dispatch.
  3. Present exact actions when policy requires human decisions.
  4. Verify decision binding, freshness and current authorization.
  5. Dispatch authorized approved intents without payload mutation.
  6. Commit effects and receipts atomically under keys/hashes.

Recover a lost response after commit

  1. Responses can be lost before local receipt checkpoints.
  2. Resume unresolved durable intents with stable identity.
  3. Look up or retry the same intent under the sink contract.
  4. Existing receipts return prior results without new effects.
  5. Verify outcomes or reconcile when lookup is unsupported.

Recall and compact context

  1. Retrieve authorized relevant facts rather than all history.
  2. Return bounded snippets with provenance, versions and uncertainty.
  3. Keep critical approvals, intents and results outside lossy summaries.
  4. Assemble current contexts under token/data policies.

Cancellation does not roll back effects

  1. Request stop and revoke further admission under policy.
  2. Persist cancellation/fencing generations and in-flight identities.
  3. Stop undispatched work without assuming remote cancellation succeeded.
  4. Reconcile in-flight receipts rather than blind replacement actions.
  5. Record prior effects or owner-assigned unresolved outcomes.

8. Durable execution and side-effect crash windows

Separate intents, effects and receipts

  1. Persist intents

    Persist stable action identities and payload hashes.

  2. Dispatch

    Revalidate authority, approvals and preconditions.

  3. External commit

    Atomically bind effects and receipts where supported.

  4. Record / reconcile

    Look up or replay receipts rather than inventing new actions.

Crashes can occur between steps, so recovery must identify crossed boundaries.

Persist task/config revisions, steps, intents, approvals, receipts and completion evidence. If recovery requires intent durability before dispatch, await the selected storage boundary. Asynchronous checkpoints can lose state on crashes; resumptions also need replay/version compatibility. In-memory state is not restart durability.

External APIs may commit before responses or checkpoints arrive. Persisted intent identities/hashes permit consistent lookup/retry, but sinks must enforce atomic receipts under sufficient retention. Unsupported APIs require suitable business keys, reconciliation or review/compensation. Checkpointing after tools does not deliver universal exactly-once effects.

9. Simulate lost responses, deduplication and cancellation

Without durable intent, recovery can create a new key that bypasses sink deduplication. Stable keys without sink enforcement can also duplicate effects. Cancellation after commit stops future dispatch but requires reconciliation; timeouts and cancellation do not undo effects. Unknown outcomes need explicit ownership rather than replacement keys or assumed failure.

10. Budgets, loop detection and concurrent admission

Bound turns, tokens, time, requests, spending and concurrency. Reserve before admission, reconcile actual usage and retain uncertain exposure. Parallel tools/child runs need atomic shared budgets or sound partitioning; checking a counter only after execution cannot prevent prior overspend.

Detect repeated failures with normalized arguments and relevant state, while recognizing legitimate bounded polling. Use progress signals, backoff, error types and cancellation. Read retries do not imply safe write retries, and repeated authorization/validation failures need correction. Escalations preserve partial work and unresolved effects.

11. Separate context, summaries, run state and memory

Working context

  • Inputs available to the current call.
  • Subject to token and selection limits.

Durable run state

  • Store intents, receipts, approvals and revisions.
  • Enforce invariants independently of summaries.

Retrieved memory

  • Scope recalled facts, preferences and episodes.
  • Check freshness, authority and provenance.

Calls use information available through the serving interface’s selected context/state. Conversation services may retain state without granting unlimited cross-run memory. Understand assembly, truncation and actual evidence. Distinguish bounded working context, lossy summaries, authoritative run state and scoped external memory.

Do not store approvals, budget ledgers or receipts only in model-written summaries. Preserve provenance, unresolved questions, revisions and revalidation needs. Recalled memory needs access, freshness, deletion and poisoning controls; old preferences do not override current authority. Token-efficient compaction can still reduce task quality.

12. Context growth and total token accounting

With base b and h new history tokens per completed turn, resending full history for n calls gives n×b+h×n(n−1)/2 total input. Quadratic growth follows those assumptions rather than a universal agent-cost law. Windows, summaries, retrieval and managed contexts alter the arithmetic; caching can change billing without shrinking logical inputs.

Outputs, tools, reasoning usage, retries, workers and storage add costs. Token totals do not directly predict latency across caching and hardware differences. Compare matched cost per successful task without hiding failures or harmful actions. The worksheet inventories inputs only.

13. MCP: interoperability, capabilities and trust

MCP uses a client–host–server JSON-RPC protocol for capabilities and context. Hosts manage clients connected to servers, whose negotiated features can include tools, resources and prompts. The examined specification defines stdio and Streamable HTTP; validate actual implementation support. Interoperability does not impose one approval UI or uniform effect semantics.

Local servers execute under host OS/sandbox permissions; remote services receive data and delegated authority under configuration. Review descriptions, results and endpoint metadata. Enforce scope, token audiences, transport authentication, endpoint safety and identity. Capability negotiation does not establish blanket user consent or resource authorization.

14. Routing, workers and evaluator loops have overhead

Routing selects specialized paths, parallel workers can shorten independent work, dynamic orchestration decomposes tasks and evaluator loops iterate against explicit criteria. These patterns can mix workflows and agents. More agents do not automatically improve accuracy; shared blind spots, handoff loss and state conflicts can degrade results.

Scope child work with contracts, provenance, budgets and cancellation. Coordinate shared writes through ownership/version/fencing, and verify outcomes rather than trusting completion claims. Measure duplicated context, synthesis, tails and recovery before choosing a topology.

15. Evaluate outcomes, trajectories and attempted violations

Fixtures need expected states, effect constraints, recovery cases, budgets and adversarial observations. Evaluate success, effect correctness, authority, duplication, blocked attempts, errors and escalation. Multiple valid trajectories may exist: grade invariants and useful progress rather than requiring one literal plan.

Inject crashes, lost responses, stale approvals, revocation, conflicts and cancellation. Report cost per success alongside failure/severity rather than hiding failed work. Expand autonomy under evidence gates with suitable stopping, reconciliation and rollback limits for irreversible effects.

    17. Practice agent-runtime design decisions

    How would you control a ticket agent that can refund?

    Combine bounded exploration with deterministic policy boundaries. Enforce ownership, eligibility, amounts, versions and approvals; persist intents and reconcile receipts. Evaluate outcomes, injection and costs before expanding autonomy.

    A refund times out: can it be retried?

    Timeouts do not establish non-commit. Reuse persisted identities under actual receipt/retention contracts, or reconcile unknown outcomes rather than generating replacement keys.

    Does checkpointing after every tool establish exactly-once effects?

    No: external commits and local checkpoints are separate. Use durable intents and effect-side replay/reconciliation contracts, stating their scope.

    Can yesterday’s approval authorize a changed amount?

    Revalidate payload, identities, resources, scope and expiry, with appropriate review for changed actions. Current authorization and business rules remain independent.

    Does budget exhaustion after a write mean nothing happened?

    No: stopping future calls does not undo effects. Return receipts and partial/unresolved state, reconcile and escalate appropriately.

    Why can extra turns sharply increase cost?

    Growing full-history inputs can add a quadratic term, but inspect actual turns, inputs, outputs, retries, caching and tools. Bounded context and caches alter the result, with compaction quality evaluated separately.

    Can a server’s read-only claim be trusted?

    Descriptions do not replace implementation and credential review. Reads can disclose data and outputs can be malicious; enforce identities, scope and boundaries.

    How do you cancel a run with an in-flight external tool?

    Persist stopping, block new work and propagate supported cancellation. Reconcile receipts and fence stale workers where supported; remote abort and compensation are separate contracts.
