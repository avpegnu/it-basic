Reliability, Resilience and Observability

Define journey-based SLOs, explore retries and dependency failures, and design containment, observation and recovery.

Reliability is a feedback loop

  1. Define good outcomes

    Journeys, invariants and SLOs.

  2. Contain failures

    Admission, isolation and degradation.

  3. Observe and respond

    Burn, traces, ownership and runbooks.

  4. Recover and learn

    Restore, reconcile and fix feedback loops.

Design objectives, signals and responses together rather than adding dashboards after the architecture.

  • SLIs must measure user outcomes; 2xx or CPU alone is insufficient.
  • Availability multiplication needs independence; replicas can fail together.
  • Jitter spreads timing; budgets, deadlines and admission bound work.
  • Breakers, bulkheads and load shedding protect different boundaries.
  • Backups, rollback and failover need recovery tests with correctness checks.

1. Start with the user journey

Correct behavior

  • Invariants must survive retries and failover.
  • Successful responses must match the promised state.

Bound the impact

  • Optional dependencies must not break the main journey.
  • Separate failure domains and roll out in small scopes.

Verified recovery

  • Provide runbooks, owners and recovery tests.
  • Restore data and reconcile post-failure effects.

A running process does not mean a user completed a task. Checkout may return 200 without saving an order; fast APIs may return permissions older than their contract permits. Define correctness, availability, latency and freshness for important operations before choosing a number of nines.

Reliability includes detection, containment, recovery and safe changes. More complexity may add failure modes and on-call burden. Consider cost, tolerable data loss and interruption rather than assuming multi-region is always better.

2. SLIs, SLOs, SLAs and measurement scope

From behavior to operational action

  1. Define a good event

    Correct, timely and eligible.

  2. Measure the SLI

    Good / total at an explicit boundary.

  3. Set SLO and budget

    Target, window and policy.

  4. Alert and reduce risk

    Burn rate, ownership and runbooks.

Targets and alerts need the same definitions/windows; machine uptime is not user-request success.

An SLI measures behavior, such as the proportion of eligible checkouts completed correctly within 500 ms. An SLO targets that SLI over a defined window; an SLA adds agreed consequences. Define exclusions before incidents rather than changing denominators to improve results.

Measure near users, such as edge/client boundaries, and correlate with backend signals. Low CPU does not prove service health when requests stall upstream. Define low-traffic, synthetic-check, disconnect and missing-telemetry policies rather than hiding failures through absent data.

3. Error budgets are not always downtime

A request-based 99.9% SLO over ten million eligible requests allows 10,000 bad requests. That is not automatically 43.2 minutes/month under variable traffic. A time-based SLI over an assumed thirty-day month yields 43.2 minutes at 0.1%. These measure different things.

Burn rate is observed bad-event ratio divided by allowed bad-event ratio. At a 99.9% target, a short window with 1% bad events burns at 10×. Multiwindow alerts address fast and slow burns; thresholds should reflect budget protection and response capability. Teams agree what budget exhaustion changes rather than automatically banning every change.

Consider a measured thirty-day period with 10,000,000 eligible requests and a 99.9% target, giving 10,000 bad events of budget. One busy hour contains 200,000 requests at 2% bad: 4,000 bad events. Its error-ratio burn is 2% / 0.1% = 20×, and it consumes 40% of the period budget. This is retrospective accounting, not a forecast of remaining traffic.

Do not call 20 × 1/720 = 2.78% the budget consumed here: the hour is 1/720 of the time but 2% of requests. Converting ratio burn through elapsed time requires suitable volume/population assumptions. Track bad-event counts and traffic mix, especially with low traffic or a large tenant dominating a short window.

Multiwindow alerts commonly require both a longer and a shorter window to exceed a branch threshold, then OR branches for fast/slow burns. The short window confirms ongoing trouble and the longer one validates material impact. Choose thresholds, sample requirements, paging/tickets and ownership for the service rather than copying universal numbers.

4. Availability math needs independence assumptions

If all dependencies must succeed and their failures are independent, success probability is the product of their availabilities. Correlated failures require joint probabilities, not multiplication of marketing figures presented as a journey SLO. Common causes can invalidate the independent estimate in different directions.

Redundancy needs failover capacity, correct routing and bounded common causes. Two regions may share identity/configuration failures or a bad deployment. Making a dependency optional can be more valuable than replicating every box.

The first controls compare separate homogeneous chains and replica groups. The composer then combines heterogeneous rows: effective_i = 1 − (1 − a_i)^r_i, multiplying required rows conditional on no common failure. A shared catastrophic event of probability c gives (1−c) times that product. This explicit conditional model does not infer independence from different region names.

5. Timeouts, deadlines and cancellation

Call timeouts must fit the remaining end-to-end deadline. Connection setup, TLS, queueing and response reads may be included or excluded by a client. Define the boundary first. Propagate remaining budgets and reject expired work; cancellation should reach workers without pretending it undoes committed transactions.

Recommendations need not be a checkout hard dependency. Give them a separate budget, bulkhead and fallback while checkout proceeds. Track degraded responses so fallback does not hide a chronic fault. Overly short timeouts can reject normal cold-start/handshake work.

Checkout: contain optional-dependency failures and overload

Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

These are logical traces, not additive latency calculations. Recommendations are assumed read-only and optional; local fallback adds no network dependency. Bounded telemetry buffers do not become response-critical dependencies.

Dependency responds within budget

  1. Admission accepts work still useful within the deadline.
  2. Start an optional read under its own budget/pool, potentially alongside core work.
  3. A result arrives within budget without becoming an order-correctness condition.
  4. Execute the contractual core transaction when resource admission permits.
  5. Receive an outcome at the promised durability boundary.
  6. Publish signals off the critical path.
  7. Return a valid core outcome with optional content where available.

Slow dependency: controlled degradation

  1. The core request passes admission with remaining deadline.
  2. Attempt an optional read without sharing the entire connection/thread pool.
  3. The read budget expires; stop waiting/cancel best effort and retain accounting until resources are actually released.
  4. Select local data or omit the widget under the approved contract.
  5. Obtain a bounded fallback without waiting for another network service.
  6. The core transaction retains all invariants; fallback must not hide a core failure.
  7. Confirm the core outcome; mutation timeouts require their own lookup/reconciliation.
  8. Count degraded responses and optional latency/failures for investigation.
  9. Return a valid core result with permitted degraded experience.

Core overload: reject early

  1. In-flight capacity/queues are full and new work cannot complete usefully.
  2. Record admission rejection without opening expensive dependency calls.
  3. Return contractual overload, such as 503, without claiming successful order creation.

6. Retries: total attempts differ from retry count

One initial attempt plus two retries is three attempts. Five layers each allowing three attempts can generate 3^5=243 leaf attempts under persistent failure. This is a worst case, not normal traffic. Place retry ownership where deadlines, errors and idempotency are understood rather than always at the outermost layer.

Retry transient failures likely to clear, not validation errors or authorization denials. Interpret 429/503 under the API’s guidance. A side-effect timeout is an unknown outcome: use idempotency, status lookup and reconciliation instead of repeating unsafe mutations.

7. Backoff, jitter and retry budgets do different jobs

Capped exponential backoff grows a delay ceiling; full jitter samples within its policy’s [0, ceiling]. It spreads synchronized retries without automatically reducing total attempts. Retry budgets instead limit admitted retries; deadlines may stop attempts even when budget remains.

Define budget scope: process, tenant, destination or fleet. Hundreds of clients each permitting extra retries still need destination capacity. During broad overload, backoff does not replace admission/load shedding. Do not wait and retry after the caller’s deadline has expired.

before each attempt:
  require retryable outcome + safe operation
  require remaining deadline and attempt budget
  require destination retry allowance
  delay = policy_backoff_with_jitter(attempt)
  honor server not-before guidance when present
  stop if the next attempt cannot fit the remaining budget

Condition pseudocode; implementations also need cancellation and protocol-specific error classification.

8. A circuit breaker does not replace timeouts

Closed permits calls and observes outcomes; open rejects early; half-open admits a bounded number of recovery probes. Choose failure/slow-call metrics, sample minimums, windows and cooldowns. Caller-caused 4xx errors do not necessarily indicate dependency failure.

Every call/probe still needs a timeout. Per-process breakers may probe together after cooldown; use suitable jitter and limits. Concurrent implementations must prevent stale responses closing a newer breaker generation and protect transition/accounting races.

9. Bulkheads, load shedding and graceful degradation

Bulkheads isolate pools/concurrency by dependency or tenant. Bound queues by memory and remaining usefulness: thousands of queued one-second requests may be abandoned work. Early rejection is cheaper than running into timeouts but requires explicit priority/fairness.

Stale fallbacks are valid only where the contract permits them. Do not degrade authorization to allow or exact balances to approximate while claiming success. Fallbacks consume resources and may share failures. Measure degraded quality alongside completed requests.

10. Metrics, logs and traces answer different questions

Metrics · Is something wrong?

  • Rates, errors, latency distributions and saturation.
  • Actionable journey/budget alerts.

Logs · What happened?

  • Structured fields, correlation and error context.
  • Controlled sampling, retention and access.

Traces · Where did time go?

  • Critical paths, dependencies and queue waits.
  • Sampling can miss failures; traces are not a census.

Metrics support aggregates/rates/alerts, logs give event detail and traces connect service boundaries. RED covers request Rate, Errors and Duration; USE covers resource Utilization, Saturation and Errors. Means help total work and Little’s Law but do not replace tail latency; quantiles need defined populations/windows.

Do not sum service p99s for end-to-end p99 or average instance p99s. Aggregate compatible histograms before deriving quantiles where appropriate, accounting for sampling and missing data. Tracing needs propagation, span lifecycles and separate queue/service time.

Keep metric labels bounded; user/request IDs can explode cardinality. Put necessary IDs in controlled traces/logs with access and retention policies. Avoid default logging of tokens, secrets or complete payloads; debugging should not undermine privacy or cost control.

start span for the logical operation
  propagate trace context through the configured client
  record bounded outcome/error class and queue/service timing
  on failure: record a redacted exception and mark error status
  finally: end the span, including exception/cancellation paths
export with bounded buffering, access and retention policy

An instrumentation lifecycle, not a standalone SDK snippet. Clients require propagation configuration; starting a span does not automatically carry context over every HTTP/message call. Redact secrets and define telemetry loss when bounded buffers fill.

11. Safe releases and conditional rollback

Roll out through validation gates

  1. Prepare contracts

    Compatibility, flags and fallbacks.

  2. Small canary

    Cohorts, baselines and guardrails.

  3. Expand by scope

    Cells, zones and regions.

  4. Validate and recover

    Deliberate rollback or reconciliation.

Stop or roll back under the SLI policy; irreversible migrations need forward-recovery plans.

Deployments, configuration and schema changes are important failure sources. Flags separate deployment from release; canaries compare cohorts before expansion. Use representative traffic and enough observation for slow burns rather than a few successful responses. Track control-plane dependencies and configuration versions.

Code rollback does not undo data/schema migrations or external effects. Use expand/contract, compatible messages, throttled backfills and reconciliation. Blue-green needs capacity and shared-state compatibility; changing a load balancer is not universal instant rollback.

12. RPO/RTO, incident response and game days

Retry feedback loop

  • Errors increase attempts, causing saturation and further errors.
  • Contain through deadlines, retry budgets and resource admission; jitter changes timing.

Cache-miss feedback loop

  • Cache loss/expiry raises origin load, causing timeouts/retries and further slowdown.
  • Bound/coalesce fills and throttle warming rather than forwarding every miss to the database together.

Reconnect feedback loop

  • Mass disconnects synchronize TLS/auth/catch-up, creating overload and more disconnects.
  • Jitter reconnects, apply tenant admission, resume cursors and bound catch-up; measure fan-out as well as sockets.

Replication is not backup: an erroneous deletion may replicate quickly. RPO describes tolerated data loss under a time/data contract; RTO targets recovery time. Restore tests need keys, permissions, dependencies, capacity, schemas and correctness checks, not merely existing backup files.

Runbooks need owners, impact assessment, safe mitigation and recovery validation. Incidents require coordination, communication and decision records. Game days need hypotheses, bounded scope, abort conditions and spare capacity, progressing through controlled failures rather than random destruction.

Cells limit blast radius only when shared dependencies, routing and deployment do not spread failures across them. After incidents, identify retry/cache-miss/reconnect feedback loops, add containment and verify with reproductions.

13. Interview drill: design failure behavior

How do you protect checkout from slow recommendations?

Make recommendations optional with its own budget/pool, minimal critical-path retries and a tested fallback. Observe degraded mode. Checkout overload still needs its own admission; the recommendations breaker does not solve it.

For an illustrative 300 ms page deadline with 80 ms already spent, 220 ms remains. Fixed example core work of 150 ms plus 20 ms rendering/cleanup leaves 50 ms margin. Optional recommendations can run in parallel with a 40 ms budget and no critical-path retries, then use local fallback/omit the widget. Adding separate p99s does not establish end-to-end p99; measure joint distributions, queues and resource contention.

Bound both in-flight and queued work; stopping a wait does not mean downstream connections/CPU were released. Test slow reads, connect/TLS delays, timeouts, open breakers and fallback failures, measuring checkout SLIs and degradation. If checkout/database is itself saturated, reject early rather than spending optional work on a response whose core operation cannot succeed.

Do five 99.9% dependencies yield 99.5%?

The product is about 99.501% only for required, independent dependencies under compatible definitions/populations. Shared causes and fallbacks change the model. Measure journey SLIs and remove avoidable hard dependencies rather than treating marketing figures as guarantees.

Do three retries at five layers mean 243 attempts?

Define terms first. Three total attempts yield 3^5=243. Three retries plus the initial attempt yield 4^5=1,024 in the worst case. Define retry ownership and end-to-end budgets to prevent uncontrolled multiplication.

Does jitter reduce request counts?

Not automatically: jitter changes timing and reduces synchronization. Attempt caps, budgets and deadlines bound counts. Spreading the same work can still overload an unrecovered dependency.

Do more replicas solve common-cause failures?

Not when replicas share broken configuration, credentials, regions or deployment pipelines. Separate failure domains and verify routing/capacity and rollout. Replicas describe topology; independence and recovery require evidence.

Does 100% 2xx establish a healthy SLO?

No. Responses can be late, incorrect or merely accepted while work never completes. Define good events around the journey and observe correctness/freshness and latency near users.

Does a successful backup prove disaster recovery readiness?

No. RPO/RTO recovery needs keys/access, compatible schemas, dependencies, capacity and invariant checks. Reconcile external side effects and validate user journeys before declaring recovery.

For an illustrative five-minute RPO and sixty-minute RTO, daily backups do not satisfy the RPO merely because copying a snapshot takes ten minutes. Restore an appropriate snapshot/log chain, with decryption/access and split-brain-safe authority. Record disruption onset, detection, decisions, provisioning, restoration, replay, invariant checks and cutover, comparing end-to-end recovery with the agreed target. Do not hide detection delay merely because the restore timer has not started. Reconcile external payments against stable intent identities/provider status rather than blindly replaying charges after restoring older database state.
