Building a Creator Video Platform: upload, discovery and delivery

Design a user-uploaded catalog with long-tail delivery, generation-safe processing, rights gates, sharded metadata, retrieval/ranking, codec economics and short-video prefetch across thirteen stages and three experiments.

Four questions for an open catalog

  1. Accept a source

    Resume, validate, finalize and retain accepted identities.

  2. Make it playable

    Baseline encoding and independent policy gates.

  3. Help discovery

    Retrieve, filter, rank and check visibility at playback.

  4. Serve the right bytes

    Compatible renditions, local caches and useful prefetch.

Track media generations and policy versions throughout the lifecycle.

  • Uploaded hours drive source/encoding/storage demand; watched seconds and bitrates drive media egress.
  • Baseline playback, optional enhancements and policy permission are distinct readiness states.
  • Rankers cannot rescue retrieval misses; measure eligible recall from evidence rather than invented recall curves.
  • Codec payback depends on compatible watched duration, matched quality and marginal costs, not view counts alone.
  • Long-tail caching and short-feed prefetch need useful-byte and cold-miss budgets rather than indiscriminate caching.

User uploads differ from a curated catalog

Creator path

  • Resumable bytes, quotas, processing status, captions/thumbnails and review workflows.
  • Upload completion does not mean public visibility or all codecs ready.

Viewer path

  • Discovery, playback admission, compatible manifests, startup/rebuffering and privacy.
  • A recommendation does not grant media access.

Operating path

  • Generations, backlogs, replication, policy propagation and deletion evidence.
  • Hot videos and upload floods need independent admission and isolation.

Assume authorized users can upload clips independently of viewing demand, with varied source formats and many rarely viewed items. Ingestion, encoding and storage therefore cannot be sized from watch requests alone. This educational architecture follows product pressures without claiming a company’s current private topology, traffic or algorithms.

Start with upload/duration/resolution limits, privacy modes, processing/first-play targets, clients, removal/review behavior and retention. The Video Platform case covers foundational ABR and processing DAGs; this episode adds catalog economics, discovery and lifecycle decisions. Do not reduce every metric to requests per second.

Define lifecycle before microservices

Separate source uploading/finalization/baseline readiness from policy pending/allow/hold/block states. Public publication references a complete generation with a valid current visibility/policy decision. Optional resolutions/codecs can arrive later instead of waiting for every task.

Finalization verifies chunks/offsets/digests and creates an immutable generation. Use outboxes/reconciliation between finalized sources and queues, with retries that do not multiply jobs. Workers retain generation/profile/attempt/lifecycle context. Versioned deletion/privacy/blocking prevents stale completions from republishing.

Thirteen stages driven by bottlenecks

Each stage exposes paths, problems, decisions, trade-offs and exit criteria. A small team need not build sharding, ISP hardware or custom encoder chips immediately. Retry identities, publication gates, bounded queues and correct removals still matter at small scale.

Upload to one playable rendition

The MVP needs resumable uploads, source finalization, one encode target, lifecycle metadata and a watch page. Avoid encoding inside long HTTP requests. Even one application can serve media through a separate object/static path rather than API-proxying every byte.

Problem pressure: Creators need a playable link after processing, before elaborate infrastructure.

Architectural decision: Use a modular control app, source storage, durable tasks and explicit processing states.

Costs and trade-offs: Accepted uploads still need repair/retry, quotas and parser isolation.

When is the next step needed?: Byte traffic and API traffic grow differently.

Resumption preserves source identity and public links never reference missing assets.

Separate bytes from pages

Control APIs return metadata/configuration and scoped references while caches serve immutable segments. Analyze hot sets by region, rendition and segment. Long-tail misses do not prove that bypassing CDNs is always cheaper; egress contracts, cache prices and origin topology determine costs.

Problem pressure: Large transfers exhaust web/socket pools despite modest CPU use.

Architectural decision: Separate media delivery with tiered caching and bounded/coalesced origin fetches.

Costs and trade-offs: Cold starts, fragmentation and deletion/cache policies need operations.

When is the next step needed?: Read-heavy metadata starts lagging or saturating.

Media bypasses application memory and miss bursts do not collapse origins.

Replicas need freshness contracts

Tune queries/indexes and pools first. Replicas can absorb stale-tolerant reads. Creator read-your-write flows need version-aware reads or appropriate primary routing. Playback/rights admission cannot use arbitrary stale replicas merely because watch pages are read-heavy.

Problem pressure: Browsing/watch metadata consumes read capacity.

Architectural decision: Classify freshness, observe lag and bound failover loss.

Costs and trade-offs: Replicas do not scale writes or automatically guarantee lossless failover.

When is the next step needed?: Thumbnails create a high-rate small-object workload.

Post-edit refreshes meet version promises and stale rights do not leak access.

Optimize tiny objects

A browse page requests many image sizes. Version thumbnail formats/qualities and cache them with bounded generation. Object services, packed blocks or key-value stores can fit measured workloads; neither per-image local files nor one particular database is mandatory.

Problem pressure: Small-file metadata/IO and transformation overhead exceed the apparent byte volume.

Architectural decision: Use predefined variants, caching/negative caching and isolated generation queues.

Costs and trade-offs: Variants add storage and arbitrary resizing can be abused.

When is the next step needed?: One primary still receives every creator’s writes.

Hot variants cache effectively and invalid dimensions cannot spawn unlimited work.

Shard by access patterns

Owner/channel locality can colocate upload settings and creator views, while global watch/search/recommendation queries need other indexes/projections. Choose keys by invariants rather than row counts alone, with explicit handling of hot creators/videos and cross-owner interactions.

Problem pressure: Write volume and metadata outgrow one machine.

Architectural decision: Use explicit shard maps, colocated transactions and searchable read models.

Costs and trade-offs: Cross-shard joins/counts cost more and replication lag does not vanish.

When is the next step needed?: Rights matching/review must keep up with uploads.

Routing failures cannot produce dual-authority writes and hot keys are guarded.

Rights/safety as a versioned lifecycle

Fingerprint matches provide evidence/claims for policy, not universal legal truth or complete infringement detection. Version decisions, territories, disputes, review and reversals. This design separates public-publication gates from encoding readiness; specific product policies require explicit definition.

Problem pressure: Valid bytes do not imply permission to publish or monetize everywhere.

Architectural decision: Separate technical readiness, policy decisions and generation-bound publication.

Costs and trade-offs: False positives, pending reviews and post-publication changes require user workflows and repair.

When is the next step needed?: Scattered application shard/pool logic becomes difficult to change.

Late workers cannot republish deleted/blocked generations and policy changes propagate.

Centralize SQL routing and query budgets

A layer such as Vitess illustrates retaining MySQL with keyspace-aware routing/planning, pooling and resharding operations. It is more than a TCP proxy and does not remove distributed SQL constraints. Define supported queries/transactions, scatter limits, schema rollout and topology availability.

Problem pressure: Services each understand shards and open excessive connections.

Architectural decision: Share routing policy, bound pools and stage fenced shard migrations.

Costs and trade-offs: The critical routing layer needs availability, observability and drills.

When is the next step needed?: Creators want live streams alongside batch uploads.

Resharding preserves ownership and global queries have deadlines and budgets.

Live is a deadline-driven pipeline

Live ingest continuously transcodes/packages aligned chunks and publishes sliding manifests. Latency includes capture, encoding, ingestion, processing, packaging, delivery and player buffers; segment duration is only one part. Reconnects, timestamp discontinuities, codec changes and ending streams need explicit protocols.

Problem pressure: A batch encode finishing minutes later cannot serve live viewers.

Architectural decision: Use streaming DAGs with stage deadlines, priorities and shared-segment coalescing.

Costs and trade-offs: Lower latency leaves less retry/buffer margin and recent segments attract herds.

When is the next step needed?: Additional codecs/renditions increase compute cost.

Ingest failures/retries preserve timelines and never publish partial media, with explicit backpressure.

Encode more when it pays back

Compare codec ladders at matched perceptual targets using device compatibility, watched seconds and egress/storage/compute costs. Publish a baseline early and prioritize expensive profiles by demand/forecasts. Do not assume every upload becomes popular or apply one published chip benchmark to your fleet.

Problem pressure: Long-tail uploads incur encoding/storage costs even with few views.

Architectural decision: Use tiered readiness and incremental codec investment with thresholds and uncertainty.

Costs and trade-offs: Additional codecs expand testing, storage and decoding/battery concerns.

When is the next step needed?: A large catalog needs effective discovery.

Verify quality/device support and make encoding cost/benefit assumptions explicit.

Retrieve then rank with recall evidence

Candidate sources can include subscriptions, similarity, collaborative signals and exploration. Deduplicate/filter/score within a budget; expensive ranking sees only retained candidates. Quality, safety and diversity are not captured by clicks or raw watch time alone; define evaluations and privacy boundaries.

Problem pressure: Scoring every item with an expensive ranker exceeds latency/cost targets.

Architectural decision: Use multi-source retrieval, bounded ranking and observable eligibility/coverage.

Costs and trade-offs: Missing candidates cannot be ranked; measure feature/model/index skew and feedback bias.

When is the next step needed?: Viewers across networks need local cache placement.

Separate retrieval recall audits from ranking metrics and prevent blocked/private item leakage.

Place hot bytes near demand

Regional/ISP caches can reduce external traffic for cacheable hot sets, depending on geography, formats, popularity and peering. Typical hit ratios are neither total egress savings nor availability guarantees. Origin shielding, coalescing, routing readiness and failure reserves still matter during cache/network loss.

Problem pressure: Peering/transit and last-mile congestion hurt QoE despite datacenter capacity.

Architectural decision: Place hot media using local evidence with tiered fallback and bounded fills.

Costs and trade-offs: Remote hardware, partners, fragmentation and correlated failover load add complexity.

When is the next step needed?: Short feeds need rapid startups across successive clips.

Cache outages do not overload upstream and byte/request hit rates are measured separately.

Feed sessions and bounded prefetch

Short-video feeds prefetch selected prefixes rather than many full clips. Bound depth by network/data settings, current playback priority and prediction confidence. Distinguish impressions, starts, watched intervals, swipes, loops and prefetch bytes; retries do not multiply events and loops are not automatically independent high-quality engagement.

Problem pressure: Cold starts delay every swipe while blind prefetch wastes data.

Architectural decision: Use session-aware feeds, prefix caches, cancellation and useful-byte telemetry.

Costs and trade-offs: Wrong predictions and rapid swipes waste bytes; personalization still needs privacy/access checks.

When is the next step needed?: Encoding becomes a recurring fleet bottleneck.

Additional depth cannot starve current playback and startup/waste follow actual paths.

Educational responsibilities, not a private production diagram.

Trace the main path

  1. Request feed batches under current sessions/cursors and data settings.
  2. Retrieve eligible next clips and bound prediction depth.
  3. Reserve current playback bandwidth before prefetching prefixes.
  4. Fetch pinned ranges/generations, cancel stale session work and measure useful bytes.

Accelerate workloads without skipping system design

For sufficiently large, stable workloads, compare CPU/GPU/video accelerators by throughput, latency, quality and total cost. Fixed-function hardware still needs scheduling, fallback, compatibility, observability and generation rollouts. Custom hardware has lead-time/design risks and is not a default startup step.

Problem pressure: New codecs and upload volume make general compute expensive.

Architectural decision: Use heterogeneous fleets with fair priorities, fallbacks and measured economics.

Costs and trade-offs: Hardware failures, placement and scarce capacity become reliability concerns.

When is the next step needed?: Improve quality, cost and safety without breaking upload/play contracts.

Benchmark matched quality/workloads, recover from failures and include idle/design/operating costs.

Size uploaded hours and watched seconds

Assume 20 source hours per minute at 12 Mbps; a baseline ladder totals 8 Mbps and an optional 4 Mbps ladder covers 25% of uploaded duration. Rates are held constant for teaching. Decimal storage excludes replication, erasure overhead, audio/captions/thumbnails and old generations.

Encoding work is not output bytes: codec, presets, complexity and chunk parallelism affect costs/deadlines. Viewer egress follows watched seconds and actual bitrates. Caches reduce upstream transfers under the topology but do not eliminate edge-to-viewer bytes. Peaks/bursts need separate headroom.

              Trace five paths

              Upload and Watch meet at immutable published media, not an application server holding every byte. Publication needs current encoding and policy/visibility evidence. Recommendations suggest identities rather than replace access checks. Removal must govern future access even while discovery projections catch up.

              Upload, publication, discovery and playback

              Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

              Media generation and policy readiness meet at publication; recommendations do not replace playback authorization.

              Upload and process

              1. Create a scoped resumable session with declared limits.
              2. Upload chunks, query offsets and finalize a verified generation.
              3. An outbox/reconciler enqueues source-generation tasks idempotently.
              4. Claim tasks with retry identities, profiles and resource budgets.
              5. Write immutable outputs and verify dependencies before readiness.

              Policy and publication

              1. Inspect sources/evidence under versioned rules and scope.
              2. Commit the selected policy decision/hold/review version.
              3. Report a complete baseline generation and integrity evidence.
              4. Publication checks lifecycle/visibility/generation/policy and cannot resurrect deletion.

              Watch page and media bytes

              1. Request watch context with device and relevant region/identity.
              2. Check visibility, policy, generation and freshness.
              3. Return playback configuration/scoped manifests without proxying media.
              4. Fetch compatible segments with buffer/network-aware ABR.
              5. Misses use tiered/coalesced upstream fetches and origin budgets.

              Retrieve, filter, rank

              1. Send bounded session/consented context.
              2. Query sources with known model/index versions.
              3. Deduplicate/check eligibility and bound the pool.
              4. Apply policy/diversity and return IDs; playback authorizes again.

              Delete or change visibility

              1. Submit an authorized versioned deletion/privacy request.
              2. Commit tombstones/current visibility and retain retry outcomes.
              3. Invalidate discovery projections with bounded repair.
              4. Apply denial/expiry and purging under declared policies.
              5. Cancel or ignore stale publication attempts using generation checks.

              Long-tail delivery, thumbnails and metadata

              Hot set

              • Local popularity and rendition distribution guide placement.
              • Coalesce misses and reserve upstream capacity for edge failures.

              Cold catalog

              • Retain reliable origins and costed tiers rather than preloading everything.
              • Rarely viewed items still need good first play, authorization and recoverable assets.

              Video metadata

              • Use shard locality/query budgets; global projections are not automatically exact or fresh.
              • Thumbnail variants and metadata versions need deliberate cache keys.

              Viral segments and many unique cold clips stress different resources. Measure byte hits, request hits, miss latency, duplicate fills and regional capacity separately. Keys pin generations/renditions/segments and relevant access context. Scope tokens without unnecessary fragmentation or private-data leakage, and bound negative caching for missing outputs.

              Thumbnails generate many small requests, making lookups, variants and transform admission important. Sharded SQL stores creator metadata while browse/search projections have their own freshness/removal behavior. Replica lag and caching follow version promises rather than using eventual consistency to dismiss privacy constraints.

              Retrieval recall needs evidence

              Rankers cannot inspect videos outside the candidate pool. Larger pools can improve recall but add feature/ranking work without guaranteeing satisfaction. Additional sources aid exploration/cold starts but need deduplication and policy eligibility. Blocked items remain ineligible even with high utility; filter budgets and playback authorization still apply.

              Evaluate recall with eligible judged/held-out items or costly sampled audits. Historical exposure creates selection bias. Monitor freshness, delayed labels, new creators and regions/groups rather than assuming a logarithmic ANN formula remains accurate as the corpus grows.

              Codec economics follows viewed seconds

              Bitrate savings at matched quality may not repay extra encoding if views are sparse, devices incompatible or storage long-lived.               Questions on decisions and failure paths

              An upload completes but is not playable: what does that mean?

              Complete bytes establish a source generation while baseline encoding, manifests and policy/visibility may still be pending. Show specific progress/errors instead of broken public links. Publish eligible baselines early and add enhancements through versioned manifests. Stable finalization/enqueue identities and reconciliation cover missing tasks.

              Should the entire catalog be cached?

              Choose from local demand, rendition mixes, byte/request hits, capacity, fill cost and miss latency rather than total item count. Retain reliable origins and tiered fallback for the tail. Compare actual topology/contracts for rare items and reserve capacity for cache failures instead of forwarding unlimited misses.

              Are more candidates always better?

              Larger pools can improve eligible recall but add ranking/feature work and selection burden. Measure recall with eligible judged/held-out targets or sampled audits, then online satisfaction and cost. The fixture shows discovery sources recovering useful candidates without changing the ranker. No universal candidate-count formula guarantees recall.

              Should a video with 20,000 views get an extra codec?

              Use compatible watched duration, matched quality, bitrate savings and marginal prices. In the fixture, three minutes, 1 Mbps saved, $0.04/GB and full compatibility yield 450 GB/$18 at 20,000 views. A $12 encode plus $3 storage leaves $3; 50% compatibility leaves a $6 loss. Other costs and uncertainty remain excluded.

              Why might depth five still leave the fifth clip cold?

              Depth expresses intent, while link capacity and time bound actual bytes. A 3.2 Mbps link reserving 1.2 Mbps leaves 2 Mbps, or 0.5 MB over two seconds: one 0.3 MB prefix and part of the second. Clip five stays cold. Deeper predictions do not create bandwidth; reserve playback and measure useful bytes.

              Does sharding or adding a proxy eliminate replica lag?

              No. Sharding may reduce load but replication can still lag or fail. Routing/planning/pooling and resharding tools do not automatically provide lossless failover or arbitrary cross-shard transactions. Read-your-write, rights freshness, hotspots and fenced migration remain design work.

              Is fingerprint matching sufficient for every publication decision?

              No. Matches are scoped evidence with disputes and false positives; missing matches do not prove safety or permission. Separate technical readiness from versioned policy/review workflows and regional/account effects. Keep current playback checks and explicit propagation/removal policies rather than deriving legal conclusions from fingerprints.

              Why are dedicated video chips not the first step?

              A stable workload must justify design, integration, operations and lead time. First optimize DAGs, priorities, codec selection and measured general hardware. Accelerators still need valid outputs, fairness, retries/fallbacks, reserves and compatibility. Benchmark matched quality/content rather than applying one setup’s gains universally.
