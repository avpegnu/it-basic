The System Design Interview Framework

Turn an ambiguous prompt into scope, invariants, estimates and a defensible architecture; practice time allocation, bottleneck analysis and design review.

One design discussion, four concrete outputs

  1. Frame the problem

    7 min · Users, scope, scale and invariants.

  2. Sketch the architecture

    12 min · APIs, data model, one write and one read.

  3. Explore the hard decisions

    18 min · Quantify, compare options and test failures.

  4. Close the design

    5 min · Summarize, identify limits and consider 10× load.

Example plan: 7 + 12 + 18 + 5 minutes, leaving a three-minute buffer in a 45-minute session. Adapt it to the problem.

  • Establish requirements and invariants before selecting a database or queue.
  • A good diagram explains requests, ownership, acknowledgements, retries and failure behavior.
  • Estimation identifies important decisions; it is not a memorized-numbers contest.
  • Every choice needs a reason, a cost and conditions that would change it.

1. What is the interviewer trying to observe?

Navigate the problem

  • Propose a scope you can finish and state what is deferred.
  • Turn “fast and large” into measurable assumptions.

Explain the engineering

  • Connect access patterns to data, bottlenecks and decisions.
  • Explain an alternative and when you would choose it.

Lead the discussion

  • Explain what you are validating; check alignment when scope changes.
  • Recognize limitations and revise when new information arrives.

System Design assesses how you handle uncertainty and turn needs into an operable system. There is no universal answer to “design chat”: a 500-person internal chat, millions of game connections and end-to-end encrypted messaging have different constraints. Asking useful questions removes unnecessary architecture before you spend time drawing.

Do not open with a list of Redis, Kafka, Kubernetes and microservices. Identify behaviors, correctness requirements and load, then use them to choose tools. Interviewers need reasoning, validation and responses to changing assumptions more than a familiar memorized diagram.

2. Allocate time intentionally

For a 45-minute session, the planner below allocates seven minutes to framing, twelve to architecture and API/data modeling, eighteen to deep dives, five to closing and a three-minute buffer. This is an example schedule, not a scoring rule. Estimate where it informs decisions and follow the interviewer’s priorities.

Keep a parking list for topics not yet explored: analytics, multi-region operation, moderation or schema evolution. Mark them outside the current scope and explain why. This differs from overlooking them; it demonstrates boundaries and time spent on the highest-impact bottleneck or invariant.

Step outputSelf-check
RequirementsWho uses it, what actions matter, what is the scale and what must never be wrong?
APIs / data modelWhat are the primary keys, access patterns, statuses and retry semantics?
ArchitectureWhere does one request go, when is it acknowledged and who owns the data?
Deep diveWhich component limits the system first and what quantitative evidence supports that?
Failure / reviewHow do slow or failed dependencies affect invariants and user experience?

Practice each phase

Frame the problem · 7 min

Agree on the required outcomes before choosing technology.

  • Restate the goal in one sentence.
  • Choose three to five user behaviors to support.
  • Record assumptions for DAU, reads/writes and data size.
  • State invariants and scoped latency/availability targets.
  • State what this session leaves outside its scope.

Sketch the architecture · 12 min

Build a coherent path from the client to authoritative data.

  • Define the main APIs and important responses.
  • Identify keys, primary queries and data ownership.
  • Walk through one write, one read and the acknowledgement point.
  • Mark synchronous work and work that may happen later.
  • Check alignment on the design before going deeper.

Explore the hard decisions · 18 min

Choose two hard problems that directly affect the requirements.

  • Estimate the load that actually reaches this component.
  • Compare at least two feasible options for this workload.
  • State the choice, its cost and what would change it.
  • Test a timeout, retry or important dependency failure.
  • Explain how invariants hold and bottlenecks are observed.

Close the design · 5 min

Leave a clear conclusion about behavior, limits and next steps.

  • Summarize the data path and key decisions in two sentences.
  • Name remaining risks and the measurements to watch.
  • Identify what changes first at ten times the load.
  • Explain an important rollout, rollback or migration.
  • Leave time for challenges and final questions.

3. Ask about behavior and measurable requirements

Functional · What does it do?

  • Actors: guests, hosts and operations.
  • Core flows: search, hold inventory, confirm/cancel.
  • Choose one write and one read to trace end to end.

Nonfunctional · How well?

  • Scale and peaks: DAU, RPS, payloads and growth.
  • Attach latency, freshness and availability to endpoints and measurement windows.
  • Invariant: one unit must not have two confirmed bookings.

Out of scope · When later?

  • Name deferred work and why: recommendations, loyalty and reporting.
  • Explore auth/billing deeply when central, while retaining security boundaries.
  • Record what would require changing the scope.

Functional requirements describe user actions: publishing, reading, searching, reserving, paying or cancelling. Nonfunctional requirements need scope: p99 latency for which endpoint, monthly or request-based availability, maximum staleness and retention. “Fast, scalable and consistent” does not select a design.

For reservations, an invariant is that the same inventory unit cannot belong to two confirmed bookings. Search availability may be a few seconds stale, but confirmation must use a concurrency-controlled hold/commit step. Calling all data eventually consistent discards the most important requirement.

When numbers are unspecified, propose assumptions and check whether they fit the problem. Keep them explicit and revisable: “I will assume ten million DAU, 20:1 reads/writes and one write region; active-active would need a separate ownership/conflict discussion.” Never present invented numbers as company facts.

4. Estimate to find architectural decision points

Numbers must lead to a decision

  1. Behavior

    DAU × operations per day.

  2. Actual load

    Average RPS → peak → fan-out.

  3. Constraint

    IOPS, bandwidth, CPU or a hot key?

  4. Decision

    Choose a mitigation and validation metrics.

This is a reasoning chain; different workloads have different limiting components.

Convert DAU × daily operations into average RPS and apply a justified peak factor. Separate reads, writes and background amplification: 100 post writes/s can generate 100,000 timeline inserts/s. Separate payload, indexes, replicas and media; bytes alone do not determine database nodes without IOPS/query benchmarks and hot-key distributions.

Calculate what changes the choice. Video uploads may be dominated by bandwidth and transcoding rather than metadata records. A URL shortener may be dominated by redirect reads and hot URLs. AI chat depends on token demand, active generations and KV budgets beyond requests/s. Explain sensitivity: which decision changes first at ten times the load?

5. Draw one request and its authoritative state

Begin with client → API → service → database. Explain identity, validation, transaction boundaries and responses. Add caches for a demonstrated read bottleneck, queues for work that may finish after acknowledgement and sharding when one node/partition cannot meet the workload. Every component must solve a stated problem.

Walk through one successful write and an immediate read. If acknowledgement precedes event processing, what does the client see? If a replica lags, is read-your-writes guaranteed? After a timeout, might the side effect already be committed? These questions expose semantics hidden by a generic database box.

Trace a booking: from the frontend to commit and acknowledgement

Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

Write and acknowledge

  1. The frontend sends a booking intent and request ID.
  2. The API forwards the validated intent with identity and deadline.
  3. The owner records a receipt and holds/commits inventory under its invariant in one transaction.
  4. The database confirms commit under the chosen durability contract.
  5. The owner returns the booking ID, status and stored result.
  6. The frontend receives success after commit and can read from the owner for fresh state.

Timeout and retry

  1. The previous response may have been lost after commit. The frontend retries the same request ID.
  2. The API preserves identity, intent and request ID for this attempt.
  3. The unique receipt yields the prior result without creating a second booking. Reusing the ID for a different intent must be rejected.
  4. The database returns the committed result or indicates that an in-progress attempt still needs checking.
  5. The owner returns the verified result to the API without a second side effect.
  6. The API returns the same booking result to the frontend. The earlier timeout does not prove that the transaction failed.
Client / FE
Keep the request ID stable when retrying the same booking intent.
API boundary
Authenticate, authorize the resource, validate input and propagate a deadline.
Booking owner
Check idempotency and coordinate a transaction protecting inventory.
Inventory + receipt
Use a suitable conditional update or lock and a unique request ID within the same transaction; commit before a success acknowledgement.

6. Deep dive through a causal chain

Make one decision testable

  1. Quantify

    Load, skew and the component’s budget.

  2. Compare options

    At least two feasible choices.

  3. Weigh trade-offs

    Latency, correctness, cost and operations.

  4. Make the choice

    What is sacrificed, and when would it change?

  5. Test failure

    Timeouts, duplicates and recovery.

If measurements are missing, state assumptions and a measurement plan; do not present estimates as benchmarks.

A strong argument connects requirements to access patterns, structures/indexes, bottlenecks and mitigations. For example: feeds retrieve recent candidates by recipient → recipient/sort-key index → push prepares timelines → celebrities create write amplification → hybrid pull handles expensive authors. Each step has a condition rather than being an isolated technology name.

Compare alternatives against consistency, latency, operations, team skills and migration. “SQL cannot scale” and “NoSQL is always fast” are not arguments. A relational database with suitable indexes/transactions may be simpler than several services; a wide-column store may fit a write-heavy pattern but requires query-driven modeling and explicit consistency semantics.

7. Break the design before finishing

Slow dependency

  • End-to-end deadlines and concurrency caps.
  • Degrade optional work; measure p99 and saturation.

Connection lost after commit

  • The outcome may be unknown; do not assume failure.
  • Check the receipt/status before creating another side effect.

Repeated request

  • Scoped idempotency keys with retention.
  • Constraints or transactions preserve the invariant.

Test three failure classes: slowness, disconnection and repetition. Slow dependencies need deadlines, concurrency limits and degradation; disconnections create uncertain outcomes; retries create duplicate effects. Pick an important write path and show how idempotency, uniqueness or reconciliation protects its invariant.

Discuss operations through user impact: SLIs, alerts, backlog age, rollback and headroom after losing a node. Disaster recovery needs RPO/RTO and restore tests, not merely backups. Basic security includes resource authorization, tenant isolation, secret handling and size/rate limits on expensive paths.

8. Close: summarize, identify limits and explain evolution

Three points for a clear ending

  1. Summarize behavior

    The main path and its invariant.

  2. Name the limits

    Bottlenecks, risks and measurements.

  3. Explain evolution

    10× load, rollout and rollback.

Leave room for challenges; the schedule does not replace the conversation.

At the end, narrate the data path instead of reading box labels. For example: “One owner confirms bookings; a transaction checks inventory and saves a request receipt before acknowledgement. Search may lag, while confirmation must protect the no-double-booking invariant.” Then state the cost and an unresolved issue.

At ten times the load, do not automatically add every technology. Identify the triggering metric, the smallest useful change and a rollback path. If contention on one popular event’s inventory is the bottleneck, random sharding alone does not distribute that inventory’s contention.

9. Design for a team to operate

Operations and ownership

  • Who is on call, which SLIs matter, and what runbooks cover common failures?
  • Which dependencies have a large blast radius, and how can they be isolated?

Cost and evolution

  • Measure storage, egress, fan-out and on-call cost against the workload.
  • Use expand/contract schema changes and throttled, reversible backfills.

A whiteboard design may be correct yet too difficult for the current team to implement or operate. Explain v1, what would invalidate it and the evidence required for v2. A new read model also needs backfill, comparison checks, traffic cutover and rollback; drawing it does not make its data available.

10. Communicate clearly and revise with new evidence

State the choice and its reason: “I will keep one write owner to avoid booking conflicts and use replicas for stale-tolerant search.” Then explain the trade-off: cross-region writes add latency and failover requires fencing. This lets the interviewer assess the decision without inferring intent from a diagram.

Distinguish knowledge, assumptions and measurements you still need. You can say, “I cannot choose the partition count without throughput and skew measurements; I would benchmark this workload and retain headroom.” A bounded correct answer is more useful than an unsupported confident number.

11. Compare vague and precise openings

An opening you cannot validate

  • “Use microservices, Redis and Kafka because the system must scale.”
  • Features, scale, invariants and component rationale remain unspecified.

An opening you can discuss

  • “Prioritize search and booking confirmation; defer loyalty and reporting.”
  • “Search may be briefly stale, but confirmation must not oversell. Assume one write region and 1000 peak confirmations/s.”
  • These are revisable assumptions rather than actual company figures.

A vague opening is: “I will use microservices, Redis and Kafka to scale, with NoSQL because traffic is large.” Without behavior, numbers or invariants, the choices cannot be assessed. Substituting another product barely changes the answer.

A clearer reservation opening is: “I will prioritize search and booking confirmation. Search may be stale by a few seconds, but confirmation must not oversell. Assume one write region and 1,000 peak confirmations/s. I will begin with inventory transactions under one owner, then examine contention and payment integration.” This enables meaningful race, failure and scaling discussion.

Titles and scope vary by company and team. Practice extending an operable design through migration, cost envelopes, ownership, rollout, isolation and cross-team lifecycle concerns. These are discussion dimensions rather than a formula assigning levels from boxes or keywords.

12. Recognize habits that obscure the design

Signals to improve

  • Drawing silently, leaving the listener to infer the rationale.
  • Naming products before establishing needs.
  • Offering one option without trade-offs.
  • Defending a choice after its assumptions change.

Concrete practice changes

  • State the decision and its reason before drawing.
  • Write down scope, an invariant and a driving measurement.
  • Compare two options against workload and failure behavior.
  • Treat challenges as new evidence and revise the affected part.

Use this comparison in practice: identify one habit you just displayed and repeat that part. One revised choice does not require abandoning the entire design.

13. Practice with a concrete rubric

Choose a case, record a 45-minute explanation and mark measurable requirements, invariants, APIs/data, one read/write path, a quantified bottleneck, retries/failures and trade-offs. Rate each as absent, vague or explained through an example. Improve the weakest dimension next time rather than memorizing another diagram.

What should you do with five minutes left?

Recap decisions through invariants and bottlenecks, check an important failure path and state the next extension with its trigger. Do not add services whose request lifecycle you cannot explain.

How can you tell you are overengineering?

Try removing any component not tied to a requirement, workload or failure boundary. Compare the simplest adequate design and its risks; complexity costs deployment, debugging and consistency work.

Exercise: which invariant should you clarify first for payments?

Distinguish charges, authorizations, captures, refunds and ledgers. A logical payment must not charge twice through retries, ledger entries must balance and amounts/currencies must stay exact. Then clarify provider semantics and reconciliation of uncertain outcomes.

Does every design need multi-region from the start?

No. Introduce it for geographic latency, availability or residency requirements. It adds replication lag, failover, conflict handling and operations; establish a single-region design and upgrade triggers when appropriate.

A prompt only says “design a photo-sharing platform.” What should the first five minutes achieve?

Propose a finishable scope and check alignment: photo upload/publication and a home feed, with follows if needed, while deferring stories, search or recommendations. Clarify clients, actors, visibility, retention and freshness. Select a write and read flow before choosing storage or queues.

Offer revisable assumptions for daily users, reads, uploads, media sizes and peaks. Derive averages, storage and egress without presenting assumptions as company facts or confusing averages with peaks. Identify likely deep dives in media publication/delivery and feed fan-out/visibility.

For illustration, assume ten million DAU, twenty daily feed API reads per active user and 0.1 daily uploads per active user. This gives 200 million feed reads/day, about 2315/s, and one million uploads/day, about 11.6/s. Two decimal MB per photo gives about 2 TB of raw daily uploads before variants, redundancy and indexes. These are illustrative averages; peaks and media delivery require separate estimates.

If scope changes, confirm the new goal and revise the affected APIs, data and estimates. A scope with privacy, invariants and measurable targets supports decisions better than a technology list. Check direction and remaining time before moving on.

Which invariant and latency target matter most for the agreed scope?

Thirty minutes in, the partition key cannot support a required query. How should you repair and explain the design?

State the concrete access-pattern mismatch: required filters and ordering, partition fan-out and correctness/freshness. Identify what remains valid and what needs revision rather than hiding the problem or redrawing everything immediately.

Compare a smaller index change with a projection or repartitioning. Indexes add write/storage costs; asynchronous projections add lag, backfills and access/deletion semantics. Choose against the requirement and remaining time, explaining why an uncaught-up projection cannot satisfy a strong freshness contract.

For live data, sketch a coherent snapshot/catch-up boundary, versions/deletions, validation and shadow comparison before cutover. Define write authority, stale routing and rollback after target writes begin. Name remaining benchmarks or reviews rather than treating one repaired assumption as a completed migration.

What read-path contract is needed when a projection is eventual but the query requires read-your-writes?
