Designing a Collaborative Editor: contexts, offline work and durable sync

Explore concurrent edits, stale offsets, deleted anchors and retries across OT/CRDT synchronization, snapshots, undo, presence and fenced document authorities.

Which contexts does an edit cross?

  1. Local intent

    Optimistic application, identities and pending work.

  2. Shared merge

    OT contexts or CRDT dependencies.

  3. Durable acceptance

    Authorization, commitment, deduplication and fencing.

  4. Recover and retain

    Snapshots, offline history, anchors and selective undo.

Connect visible state to acceptance and recovery evidence.

  • Convergence, intent and durable acceptance are distinct requirements.
  • OT requires compatible contexts and client protocols beyond one function.
  • CRDT merging does not replace authorization/schema/resource/retention policies.
  • Snapshots retain full merge/recovery state and compaction respects anchors.
  • Hot-document fan-out, presence and recovery need explicit ownership.

1. Convergence, editing intent and saved state

Local view

  • Confirmed state plus pending edits and explicit saved status.

Shared correctness

  • Compatible contexts/identities, convergence and intent tests.

Service guarantees

  • Authorization, durable acknowledgements, ownership and recovery.

The plain-text baseline supports concurrent edits, optimistic typing, remote updates, presence, reconnects, sharing and history. Rich text adds schemas/formatting ranges, canvases add object/tree/property semantics and spreadsheets add dependencies/formulas. Replacing strings with JSON does not solve those models. This operational baseline has a document authority without claiming every CRDT requires one.

Three goals differ: convergence after exchanging accepted updates, sensible intent/rules and survival of acknowledged changes under the stated failures. Identical wrong text remains wrong, and local display does not prove saving. Optimistic application removes network RTT from immediate feedback but not input/IME/rendering costs. Define separate local, remote, durability and recovery objectives.

2. Count batches, operations and fan-out separately

Assume 10 million peak connections, 20% typing, 250 ms batches and 250-byte batches. Peak traffic is 8 million batches/s and 2 GB/s of payload. Batches can contain multiple atomic edits, so distinguish the units. A separate daily scenario of 100 million users ×1,000 batches ×250 bytes yields 25 TB before framing/indexes/replicas. An hour at peak yields 7.2 TB; do not confuse peak extrapolation with the daily assumptions.

If workload-specific benchmarks support 100,000 sockets per gateway, 100 nodes is only a pre-reserve sizing floor. TLS/socket memory, reconnect storms, message rates, regional failover and hot documents can require more. Low average editor counts do not protect hot documents. Bound document sizes, operations, metadata and retention; benchmark transform/merge work against size and queue tails.

3. API contexts depend on OT or CRDT backends

Loads need coherent snapshots/cuts and gap-free tail/subscription handoffs. OT submissions carry confirmed revisions, stable client/update identities and compatible change sets. CRDT submissions use library-specific updates and protocol/document generations; state vectors are not global revision numbers. Server cursors may serve audit/recovery without replacing causal metadata. Do not pretend arbitrary positional JSON is interchangeable with CRDT updates.

The same ID/payload returns retained results, while changed payloads conflict. Scope identities by document/generation/client instance with explicit retention; a user ID is not a universal device clock namespace. Durable acknowledgement differs from transport receipt, and lost replies retry original identities. Expired contexts or incompatible schemas require explicit resync paths rather than clamping offsets into current text.

            4. Separate authority, content and presence ownership

            Owners hold live models, validate operations and persist accepted updates. Directories route documents/epochs, while storage/output boundaries reject stale owners. Expiring leases do not stop old processes, and consistent hashing is a routing/placement hint. Ownership needs acquisition, fencing and recovery cuts. Uncertain authority keeps edits pending or enters the designed read-only mode rather than acknowledging divergent histories.

            OT uses authoritative ordered revisions. CRDT services can centralize authorization/durability/fan-out while merge correctness still comes from library semantics. Separate ephemeral awareness from durable content so cursor floods cannot displace accepted changes. ACLs and snapshot manifests also need explicit consistency; caches are not automatically authorities.

            Optimistic local views and durable shared history

            Component map; the complete ordered request paths are explained below.

            This baseline chooses one fenced authority per document. OT uses ordered revisions; CRDT can use the same durable service without depending on its total order for merging. Presence is separate from the content log.

            Save an edit

            1. Update local views and submit stable IDs with backend-specific contexts.
            2. Route by document/generation; stale-route retries retain business identities.
            3. Check rights/schemas under revocation contracts rather than client-supplied roles.
            4. Commit accepted updates/results under the current epoch.
            5. Confirm durable cuts under the stated failure model.
            6. Return acknowledgements/remote streams; clients rebase pending work appropriately.

            Open and catch up

            1. Join with authorization and confirmed contexts or state vectors.
            2. Look up/acquire owners through epoch protocols.
            3. Join current owners and establish loading barriers.
            4. Load full snapshots at S, including required merge metadata.
            5. Read contiguous suffixes after S and attach gap-free live subscriptions.
            6. Return snapshots/tails and resync policies for expired contexts.

            Snapshot and compact

            1. Read exact cuts with compatible schema/merge/dedup state.
            2. Write immutable candidates and verify integrity/replay equivalence.
            3. Publish verified manifests before trimming and retain history needed by offline/undo/version policies.

            Keep hot-document viewers off the mutation owner

            1. Emit committed projections with generations/cuts.
            2. Deliver batched updates after snapshot barriers; slow viewers catch up/resync.
            3. Send bounded ephemeral awareness within authorization scope.
            4. Publish counts/avatars or selected cursors under scale/privacy policies.

            Recover owners and retry

            1. Grant new epochs and fence old authorities at storage/output boundaries.
            2. Restore complete snapshots and compatible versions.
            3. Replay accepted suffixes including retained identities/results.
            4. Resend unacknowledged identities and reconcile rejected/expired contexts while preserving local work.

            5. OT changes operation context before remote application

            From CODE, A inserts X at index 1 while B deletes O at index 1. Applying B unchanged after A deletes X. Transform B to index 2 after the insertion; on the reverse path A remains at index 1. Both produce CXDE. Concurrent deletes of O make the second deletion a no-op instead of deleting the new character at that offset. Same-position inserts need deterministic protocol tie-breaks.

            Pair transformations require compatible contexts. Two-operation convergence does not prove arbitrary histories, rich-text attributes or delivery schedules correct. The lab limits each client to one edit and checks both orders. Production needs compatible algorithms and client/server protocols rather than a small index-arithmetic function alone.

            6. Confirmed bases, outstanding work and buffers must stay compatible

            Track context throughout the lifecycle

            1. Confirmed base

              Exact known revisions/model generations.

            2. Optimistic queue

              Outstanding/buffered work retains identities/contexts.

            3. Remote rebase

              Rebase both directions under the protocol.

            4. Durable result

              Acknowledge identities and preserve unaccepted work.

            Local views can be ahead of confirmed shared state.

            A common protocol tracks confirmed revisions, submitted/outstanding work and buffered local edits. Typing is optimistic. Remote updates rebase through pending changes while pending changes rebase through remote updates under the selected algorithm. Acknowledgements reconcile identities/revisions before subsequent sends. Sending bytes or observing newer revisions does not justify dropping pending work. Batch composition must also preserve contexts.

            Servers transform through retained relevant history or explicitly reject expired contexts. Supported rebasing avoids endless stale-revision retry starvation, but missing history cannot be replaced with guesses. Gaps need catch-up and duplicates need deduplication. Saved/offline/error indicators follow acceptance state, and rejected work needs preservation/export/fork options.

            7. CRDT identities and merge rules do not replace application rules

            Sequence CRDTs commonly retain stable identities, ordering/origin metadata and deletion information instead of only offsets. Updates may precede dependencies, which implementations must retain/resolve. Valid uniquely identified updates can tolerate reordering/duplicates after eventual delivery, but lost updates, forged IDs, schema violations and destructive compaction are outside that guarantee. Representations vary and may use compressed runs/blocks rather than literal per-character nodes.

            Libraries solve algorithmic pieces without automatically solving permissions, schemas, limits, intent or undo. Server-backed OT also needs intent testing. Concurrent runs can interleave poorly depending on algorithms/constraints; test realistic edits. Update algebra and practical rich-text semantics are separate layers. Reusing actor identities with reset counters can create collisions.

            8. Deleting visible content differs from discarding structure

            Offline inserts can reference concurrently deleted elements. Retained structural identities/deletion information preserve their neighborhood, while visible strings alone can lose anchors/causal state. Safe compaction can retain compressed structure/redirects or establish causal stability; absence from online sessions does not prove old updates cannot return.

            Offline windows require explicit epoch transitions: old updates cannot merge blindly into new generations, and clients need resync/reconciliation policies. Never force stale offsets into new snapshots. Reclaiming payloads, retaining merge identities and keeping historical versions are distinct contracts. The deliberately broken drop-anchor lab is not a description of every CRDT garbage collector.

            9. Choose OT or CRDT for the workload and ownership model

            Central rich-text products can use mature OT stacks, while offline-first/multi-peer products may favor mature CRDT stacks. Both require synchronization, persistence and acceptance policies. Centralization adds cross-region acknowledgement costs; CRDTs still incur metadata, validation and semantic risks. Evaluate libraries with actual schemas, bindings and offline workloads rather than novelty.

            10. Durable acknowledgements, retries and owner fencing

            Acknowledgements follow recoverable commitment under a stated failure model, retaining identities/results to avoid duplicate effects. Local fsync and replicated logs cover different failures; unpublished RAM snapshots are not durability evidence. Lost replies after commits are ambiguous. Conditional revisions/generations and fenced appends prevent stale owners from writing after transfers; publication boundaries also need authority checks.

            The lab separates persistence-before-acknowledgement, lost replies and fencing. Early acknowledgements can leave clients with no pending work after server loss. Lost replies after commits recover retained results under original identities. Text-only snapshots that omit identity state can duplicate retried edits. Durability, idempotency and ownership are separate invariants.

            11. Publish exact-cut snapshots before trimming history

            Snapshots include complete backend state, versions, generations, exact cuts and integrity, with compatible recovery of metadata/deduplication. Workers fold coherent cuts into immutable candidates, verify before conditional manifest publication and never expose missing bytes. New owners load S and contiguous later updates, using live-subscription barriers to avoid load-time gaps.

            Trim under verified snapshots, retained versions, offline/rebase windows and undo requirements rather than upload completion alone. Named versions pin committed state rather than optimistic drafts. Restoration is an authorized new history action, using model-compatible transactions or coordinated generations. Never reset revisions/reuse identities; reconcile in-flight/offline work and preserve local forks when needed.

            12. Presence, anchors and undo have distinct lifecycles

            Presence

            • Throttled, ephemeral, scoped and expiry-aware.

            Comments/cursors

            • Relative anchors, affinity and unresolved-target policies.

            Selective undo

            • Scoped to local origins and user transaction grouping.

            Presence is ephemeral session state with heartbeats/expiry, throttling and audience scopes; disconnections are detected with delays. This baseline excludes cursor moves from durable content logs while allowing separate security audits. Absolute offsets drift; use transformed positions or relative anchors with explicit side affinity. Missing targets can make anchors unresolved, requiring orphan/reattachment policies for comments.

            My undo must not rewind the whole document over your edits. Track local origins/transactions, grouping and compatible selective/inverse operations against current state. Undoing deletion has explicit identity/position semantics rather than globally clearing tombstones. IME, paste and user-action grouping require correct bindings because atomic operations and user undo steps are not identical.

            13. Rich text and permission changes affect acceptance

            Rich text adds nesting, marks/ranges, embedded references and schema migrations. Valid binary merging does not establish semantic validity or safe rendering. Sanitize output, validate capabilities/bounds and use compatible versions. Malformed updates can consume resources before decoding finishes, so bound frames, decompressed bytes, operation counts, depths and tenant work rather than final text length alone.

            Joining ACL checks do not cover later revocations on open sockets. Define boundaries such as serialized ACL-version checks at admission/commit with compatible cache fencing. Offline changes can merge correctly yet fail current authorization; reject while preserving work under export/fork policies. Closing sockets cannot recall received bytes. E2EE limits plaintext/schema validation without eliminating metadata access control, key distribution or revocation limits.

            14. Hot documents often exhaust fan-out before mutation rates

            Five editors at four batches/s create only 20 incoming batches/s, but 5,000 sessions produce 99,980 naive deliveries/s or roughly 25 MB/s at 250 bytes before framing. All 5,000 sessions sending 2 Hz cursors produce nearly 50 million deliveries/s. Restricting publishers to five editors yields 49,990 deliveries/s, with further audience/viewport limits or viewer counts/avatars. These are calculations, not benchmarks.

            Separate mutation owners from read relays, batch projections and bound subscriber queues. Dropped content deltas require catch-up/snapshots rather than pretending clients remain current; ephemeral updates can be coalesced under their policies. Editor caps, large-document budgets and read-only fallback limit blast radii. Paragraph sharding still needs cross-boundary formatting/selection/undo semantics.

            15. Regions, observability and convergence evidence

            Placing owners near active editors reduces some RTTs, while remote editors still pay acknowledgement/visibility costs. Migrations require fencing and exact state/cut transfer with stable retry identities. Asynchronous regional copies have explicit RPO/lag and cannot promise unprotected failure survival. CRDT multi-writer merging does not remove authorization, durability, schema or epoch policies.

            Measure pending age, acknowledgements, visibility, acceptance/rejection/retries, replay lag, fan-out queues, presence and merge/metadata costs. Shadow replay and convergence tests expose some divergence without proving intent. Inject commit/ACK crashes, reordered/duplicate updates, expired contexts, stale owners, revocations, Unicode/IME and malformed payloads. Read-only kill switches must preserve local drafts.

            16. Practice contexts and failure boundaries

            Draw optimistic editing, a lost reply, trimmed history and an offline client returning after revocation. Identify local text, accepted shared state, pending identities, current rights and recovery policies at each step.

            Does identical replica text establish correctness?

            No. Stale concurrent deletes can converge while removing the next character too. The naive lab produces CE instead of CDE. Test convergence, targeted rules/intent, durability and authorization separately.

            Is server-only transformation sufficient for OT?

            Not when optimistic clients have pending work. Remote and pending contexts need compatible rebasing with acknowledgement/retry/history handling. Pairwise transformations alone are not a complete protocol.

            Does a CRDT remove authorization services?

            No. Merge validity differs from permission. Revoked/offline clients still face acceptance policies and resource/schema validation. Peer/E2EE topologies change enforcement rather than solve authorization automatically.

            Why can visible-text snapshots fail offline merges?

            It may omit identities, origins/deletions and causal state referenced by offline updates. Retain complete backend state or use explicit epoch/resync policies preserving work rather than guessing offsets.

            How should clients retry after lost commit acknowledgements?

            Retain original identities/payloads and pending state, then recover results through reconnect/catch-up. Durable recovery must include accepted identities; fresh IDs can create duplicate effects.

            Is undo just loading an older snapshot?

            Not for selective local undo. Track origins/user transactions and compatible undo while preserving remote work. Named-version restoration is a distinct history/epoch/reconciliation action.

            Do 5,000 viewers require sharding text mutation?

            Not necessarily: few editors may leave fan-out/presence dominant. Use relays, batching, bounded catch-up and smaller cursor audiences before fragmenting document semantics.

            Does a lease timeout guarantee old owners stop writing?

            No. Partitioned processes may continue. Enforce epochs at storage/output boundaries and restore exact committed cuts; uncertain authority must not acknowledge conflicting histories.
