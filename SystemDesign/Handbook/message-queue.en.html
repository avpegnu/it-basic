1. Design the broker rather than merely use a queue

Ordering contract

  • Append order within a partition; business causality needs additional protocols.

Durability contract

  • ACK boundaries, eligibility, fencing and declared failures must agree.

Replay contract

  • Retention, compaction holes, cursors and sink cooperation affect recovery.

The Messaging lesson explains application use of queues/streams. Here we design the log service itself: producers append to topic partitions, while independent consumer groups replay within retention windows. The baseline provides per-partition append order, acceptance under stated failure assumptions, bounded fetch, group cursors, replication/failover and tenant controls. Delays, priorities, global order and arbitrary exactly-once effects are separate requirements.

Define ACK latency by batch/ACK mode, fetch latency with data and long polls, data-loss guarantees by failure scenario, recovery time and supported lag. Durability claims distinguish broker, rack, zone and correlated power failures. Page-cache appends are not per-record fsyncs; replication across failure domains does not replace every persistence and backup policy.

2. Size ingress, copies, retention and replay separately

Assume one million records/second, 1,000 bytes each before compression/framing, RF=3, seven-day retention and three full-stream groups. Logical ingress is 1 GB/s; aggregate replica appends are 3 GB/s, with about 2 GB/s of follower replication beyond producer ingress. Consumer payload egress is about 3 GB/s. Raw replicated retention is 1,000,000 ×1,000 ×604,800 ×3 = 1.8144 decimal PB. These are arithmetic assumptions, not benchmarks or deployment recommendations.

A hypothetical 10 MB/s logical ingress budget per partition implies at least 100 partitions on average, subject to hot keys, peaks, CPU, overhead and consumer parallelism. A hypothetical 100 MB/s replica-write budget per node gives a 30-node bandwidth floor. At only 30 TB usable retained capacity/node, the capacity floor is ceil(1,814.4/30)=61 nodes before reserves/rebuilds. Size all constraints and failure-domain loss; throughput alone does not establish disk sufficiency.

Measure compression on representative batches and reserve for indexes, headers, compaction amplification, temporary files, retries, replay and recovery. Rebuilding 10 TB at an effective 200 MB/s takes at least 50,000 seconds, roughly 13.9 hours, before competing traffic and throttles. Plan failure tolerance during repair, not only steady-state operation.

3. Protocols expose meaningful offsets, epochs and errors

These logical operations define contracts rather than HTTP endpoints or Kafka-compatible wire formats. Metadata discovery returns owners/epochs and refresh rules. Produce requests identify partitions, batches, producer epochs/sequences, ACK requirements and timeouts. Brokers enforce authorization, quotas, size/checksum bounds and ownership; timeouts can leave an append outcome unknown.

Fetches specify offsets, isolation, byte/record bounds and wait/minimum-byte rules, including progress with oversized batches. Offsets are logical positions; controls, compaction and transaction filtering create gaps. Offset commits describe safely processed next positions, not merely fetched records. Stale epochs, expired offsets, insufficient replicas and quotas require different recovery policies.

            4. Separate metadata authority from partition data planes

            A metadata quorum owns configurations, placement, epochs and ownership transitions. Leaders/followers handle partition data, while group/transaction coordinators shard their own durable state. Cache discovery without permitting stale authority. Metadata consensus does not imply that each data partition uses the same replication algorithm.

            Authorized bounded requests reach current owners and carry epochs. Replica progress establishes protocol-specific visibility, while maintenance has budgets that protect foreground I/O. Select produce, consume, election, transaction or tiering traces and inspect each component to follow the boundaries.

            3. Coordinate durable decisions and recoverable commit/abort markers.

            Compact, tier and reclaim

            2. Upload complete immutable segment/index sets before publication.
            3. Commit lifecycle/version metadata before eviction while protecting readers.

            An index anchors scanning without promising that a requested record survives.

            Partitions have active and closed segments under size/time policies. Lengths, checksums, versions, timestamps and offsets protect framing; compression/batching prevents fixed offset-to-byte arithmetic. Sparse indexes anchor scans, and timestamp indexes need explicit timestamp semantics. Page caches and batching improve efficiency without eliminating storage stalls.

            Crash recovery validates framing/checksums, truncates incomplete tails under explicit rules and reconstructs indexes where possible. File existence does not establish commit. Epoch-aware reconciliation distinguishes divergent suffixes from valid history, while deletion/replacement protects active readers.

            6. Replication policies define ACKs and failover

            Do not merge majority consensus and ISR replication into one universal formula. ISR-style systems track eligibility through progress/membership. An all-replica ACK does not mean waiting for exactly the minimum count; the threshold and successful boundary have different roles. Membership changes can leave appended bytes behind failed/timed-out requests, requiring idempotent retries and explicit uncertainty.

            The lab is an ISR-only fixture: C leaves before an append, all ACKs wait for A/B even at minimum one, and minimum three rejects admission. B preserves the prefix after A fails; forcing stale C after A/B failures loses acknowledged data. Clean policies may require unavailability. The displayed HW precedes unsafe election rather than promising preservation after intentionally unsafe recovery.

            Production semantics depend on versions/configurations. ELR-enabled designs may preserve safe leader candidates outside current ISR using durable evidence, so current membership alone is insufficient to judge every implementation. This lab is not a complete Kafka model. Placement, persistence, corruption handling and repairs remain part of the durability contract.

            7. Epochs, reconciliation and retry identities

            Ownership

            • Leader epochs fence obsolete writing authority.

            Retry identity

            • Stable producer sequences deduplicate within supported scopes.

            Recovery proof

            • Recovery needs sufficient deduplication and commit metadata before serving.

            Failover requires authoritative ownership and fencing, not just a new process. Replicas/clients reject obsolete epochs; new owners reconcile divergent suffixes. Idempotence needs producer identity, epochs, per-partition sequences and recoverable bounded deduplication state. New arbitrary identities do not automatically deduplicate old business events.

            Lost ACKs require documented identity-preserving retries and in-flight rules. Brokers distinguish accepted retries, expected sequences and obsolete identities. Expired state or changed identities need explicit uncertainty rather than unlimited deduplication claims. Applications retain business identities and sink safeguards.

            8. HW, LSO and read_committed are distinct

            Use next-offset boundaries consistently: LEO follows local appends, while HW bounds the chosen protocol’s replicated/committed prefix. Transactions impose an additional stability boundary. Here LSO is the first unstable offset, or HW when no open transaction holds the prefix. Do not confuse next boundaries with last-readable record numbers.

            An open transaction at offset one can hold an interleaved replicated normal record at two. Durable resolution advances stability; committed reads filter aborted data but retain normal records. Controls are not business payloads, and uncommitted isolation does not authorize arbitrary corrupt/local bytes. Long transactions can create lag and pressure despite healthy processes.

            The simulation selects alternative histories rather than edit an existing log: offset four is an unrelated control record in the open history, or TX-A’s end marker in commit/abort histories, followed by N5. A selected decision at HW four cannot resolve an unreplicated marker; HW five can. This per-partition fixture omits distributed coordinator races and timeout services. Atomicity depends on cooperating participants and reader isolation.

            9. Consumer ownership, progress and effects

            A meaningful processing checkpoint

            1. Fetch bounded work

              Track ownership generations and record identities.

            2. Apply safely

              Use sink idempotency/fencing or atomic transactions.

            3. Advance frontier

              Advance only across completed safe effects.

            4. Commit and recover

              Persist next offsets and support crash replay.

            Fetched positions, completed work and durable commits are distinct states.

            Groups assign current partition owners while independent groups read separately. Heartbeats suspect failures without proving that old workers stopped external calls. Rebalances need revocation/cancellation and generation checks. Fencing offset commits alone cannot fence obsolete database/API effects.

            Parallel completion requires a safe contiguous progress frontier, not the highest finished offset. Interpret holes according to fetched records and isolation rather than wait for nonexistent tasks. Committing before effects risks omission; committing afterwards risks replay. Use sink idempotency or cooperating atomic checkpoints. Arbitrary HTTP charges are outside a log transaction.

            Retry topics/DLQs need ordering and recovery policies. Moving a failed event aside while applying successors can violate key state machines. Pausing partitions, isolating keys and remediation each have costs. Monitor lag age, byte distance and retention risk rather than counts alone.

            10. Retention and compaction make different promises

            Deletion retention removes closed segments under time/size policies rather than exact per-record deadlines. Compaction eventually keeps useful latest key values, with duplicate versions in uncleaned regions. It is not immediate uniqueness, does not renumber surviving offsets and does not preserve full history. Combined compaction/deletion can expire latest values as well, affecting rebuild guarantees.

            Tombstones express deletes and have retention policies. A stale local snapshot can miss an expired tombstone and retain deleted values. Supported scan windows, versioned snapshots and explicit replacement/reset policies prevent resurrection. Compaction holes seek to surviving records; offsets below log start need explicit recovery.

            Compactors rewrite eligible segments with indexes/checksums, publish generations coherently and retire old files after readers release them. Budget temporary space and rewrite I/O. Key distributions and cleaner lag affect results; compaction does not immediately shrink storage or erase every replica/backup.

            11. Tiered storage needs manifests and lifecycle protocols

            Publish

            • Complete bytes/indexes precede committed readable manifests.

            Serve

            • Budget cached/range reads separately for catch-up and live fetches.

            Reclaim

            • Lifecycle metadata and reader pins determine safe eviction/reclamation.

            Upload closed immutable segments with indexes/checksums and scoped identities. Publish metadata only after required objects are complete/readable; evict local files after durable metadata and reader/lifecycle checks. Partial uploads are not replayable published segments, and listings alone should not define committed history.

            Cold fetches need indexes, ranges, caches and quotas that protect live traffic. Tiering reduces local capacity pressure without removing ingress, replication, metadata, egress and recovery costs. Generation-aware lifecycle rules prevent dangling manifests, premature reclamation and inconsistent deletion policies across tiers.

            12. Batching, compression and backpressure need bounds

            Partition batching amortizes syscalls, round trips and compression, trading linger latency against efficiency. Bound in-flight work, retries, memory and delivery deadlines. Brokers need fair bounded queues and tenant quotas. Compression trades bytes for CPU and requires expanded-size limits.

            Long polling reduces empty fetch churn under bounded waits and cancellation. Zero-copy paths may reduce copies, but TLS, transformations and conversion affect applicability; CPU work does not disappear. Separate replication, cleaning, reassignment and replay budgets protect ACK latency.

            Sustained arrival above service capacity grows backlog until retention or quotas intervene. Scaling is constrained by partitions, ordering, sinks and rebalances. Service-time and byte metrics reveal whether extra workers help or increase contention.

            13. Hot keys, reassignment and regional boundaries

            Key routing depends on partitioner versions. Increasing partition counts may remap keys and split ordered histories. Strict continuity needs routing epochs, barriers, stable virtual shards or justified drain/cutover procedures. A serialized hot key remains a bottleneck unless the domain permits sharding/merging.

            Reassignment copies and catches up data before publishing membership and retiring old replicas. Budget I/O and availability reserves to avoid eligibility churn. Balance traffic/bytes as well as counts. Existing owners may serve during controller outages under supported conditions, while elections and changes still depend on metadata authority.

            Asynchronous cross-region mirroring has lag, offsets/group translation and identity/failback concerns. Synchronous placement trades latency and availability. Active-active needs explicit order/conflict semantics rather than two writable copies. Rehearse recovered prefixes, replay duplicates and ownership cutovers.

            14. Tenancy, quotas and sensitive data

            Authenticate clients and authorize topic/group/admin operations. Names are not capabilities. Protect transport/storage and credentials while avoiding payload leaks. Schema compatibility requires versioned producer/consumer governance; broker acceptance alone does not validate business meaning.

            Quota bytes, requests, partitions, connections, storage and replay with clear throttling. Stronger isolation may require dedicated pools/clusters. Payload sizes, decompression, key cardinality, long transactions and metadata churn need bounds. Deletion policies cover replicas, tiers, snapshots and backups rather than claim instantaneous universal erasure.

            15. Observe invariants and rehearse failures

            Measure ACK latency/outcomes, replica availability/eligibility, leadership churn, disk service times/capacity, replication, metadata health, open transactions and maintenance backlogs. Track consumer lag in counts, bytes, age and retention distance. A stalled committed reader can indicate open transactions rather than replication failure.

            Test leader crashes around ACKs, lost replies, eligibility changes, stale owners, corrupt tails/indexes, full disks, stale rebalance effects, coordinator restarts and expired history/tiering failures. Assert accepted-prefix guarantees, fencing, bounds and recovery states instead of equating liveness with correctness.

            16. Practice with offsets and failure boundaries

            Start with a batch at offset four on A/B, stale C and a lost reply. Explain producer knowledge, election eligibility, visibility and consumer effects. Then introduce an open transaction and expired tombstones to connect otherwise distinct boundaries.

            Does minimum two mean all ACKs always wait for only two copies?

            No. Thresholds differ from actual ACK rules. All waits for current eligible members in this fixture; production semantics depend on the protocol/configuration.

            Can a timed-out producer safely retry with a new random identity?

            An append may already have succeeded. Retain supported identity/sequence scopes and business IDs, with reconciliation when those scopes expire.

            Does HW six guarantee every earlier record is returned to committed readers?

            No. Stability boundaries, aborted data and control records impose additional visibility rules.

            Why can a seek to offset five return record six?

            Compaction preserves logical gaps. Seeking surviving records differs from expired history, which needs reset policies.

            Does tombstone cleanup automatically fix stale local caches?

            No. Stale clients need supported catch-up windows or coherent replacement snapshots and compatible tails.

            Does single group ownership guarantee exactly-once external effects?

            No. Obsolete workers and effect/commit crashes require sink cooperation; generations protect only operations that enforce them.

            Do bandwidth-derived partitions/node counts finish sizing?

            No. Retention alone can dominate bandwidth sizing; include CPU, network, skew, replay, repair and measured per-node limits.

            Does tiering eliminate replication and metadata authority?

            No. Remote capacity still needs ownership, durability, coherent manifests, indexes and safe bounded retrieval.
